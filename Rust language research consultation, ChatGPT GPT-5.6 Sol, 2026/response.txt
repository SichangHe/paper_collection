> “Give opinions, closest-work objections, narrower experiments, and which proposal to stop.”

My ranking is: **1 strongest research bet; 4 best cheap falsification experiment; 3 viable only after a substantial narrowing; stop 2 as a standalone proposal.**

The important change from your draft is that recent work makes proposals 2 and 3 materially more crowded than the listed blockers suggest. I found no comparable new blocker that kills the narrower versions of 1 or 4. I did not rerun or independently verify every literature claim you supplied; I treated those verified-paper summaries as premises and searched particularly for missing 2024–2026 blockers.

1. cancellation that loses work despite valid memory — keep, but change the claimed gap

Opinion: this has the highest upside because the failure oracle can be an ordinary externally visible systems invariant rather than a Rust memory-model judgment. But “external effects” or even “composed cancellation correctness” cannot be the novelty.

The closest-work objection is stronger than your text currently states. Oxide explicitly defines cancel correctness globally and already discusses external state: “Cancellations that affect state outside of the process are especially fraught.” Its zone-deletion case says cancellation could leave “zones (which are external to the process) in an inconsistent state.” It also discusses interrupted database transactions and cleaning a reused connection. [Oxide Computer Company+2](https://rfd.shared.oxide.computer/rfd/0400)

So I would not claim:

> cancellation testing for remote effects

I would test a much narrower proposition:

**Can systematic replay of an operation across cancel → recreate/reconnect → retry expose violations of a separately specified end-to-end history property that existing cancellation-safety mechanisms do not detect?**

The smallest useful experiment is one historical byte-stream bug and one request/reply bug. Instrument only externally observable events: bytes accepted, request identifier, remote commit, response/acknowledgment, cancellation, retry, connection return/reuse. Enumerate reachable cancellation points around those events and check a contract written before seeing test results.

Be careful with “exactly once.” After “remote committed, response lost,” a client generally cannot distinguish commit from non-commit without protocol support. Oxide itself notes that “an operation that’s idempotent can be retried.” [Oxide Computer Company](https://rfd.shared.oxide.computer/rfd/0400) Your oracle should therefore be protocol-specific: e.g. no acknowledged bytes disappear; no duplicate effect when an idempotency key promises deduplication; reused connections satisfy the protocol's clean-state invariant.

The convincing result is not more cancellation failures than Loom. It is **a historical or new reproducible failure that ordinary scheduling, direct cancellation injection, and progress-preserving adapters all miss because the violation appears only across attempts or across an external state boundary**. If the first two real examples are already caught naturally by an existing cancellation harness, narrow again or stop.

1. safe callers that invalidate unsafe-library assumptions — stop as a proposal

Opinion: this is now too crowded at the level presently stated.

Your Crabtree/SyRust/Rudra objections were already serious. ECOOP 2025 RUXt adds a more fundamental one: for internally unsafe libraries, its theorem says detected UB “can be triggered by a safe program,” and the tool “produces a safe program witnessing the UB.” [ECOOP 2025](https://2025.ecoop.org/details/ecoop-2025-technical-papers/39/Compositional-Bug-Detection-for-Internally-Unsafe-Libraries-A-Logical-Approach-to-Ty)

There is an important limitation: RUXt's implementation analyzes “a small model of Rust,” not production Rust. [ECOOP 2025](https://2025.ecoop.org/details/ecoop-2025-technical-papers/39/Compositional-Bug-Detection-for-Internally-Unsafe-Libraries-A-Logical-Approach-to-Ty) Thus RUXt does **not** subsume a practical Miri-backed generator for real crates. But it does remove much of the conceptual contribution “generate a legal safe client demonstrating unsoundness.”

The only version I would retain is a disposable novelty probe, not a project:

**Choose exactly one adversarial caller behavior that Crabtree cannot synthesize and RUXt does not model—for example a user-defined trait implementation combining reentrancy with destructor or unwind behavior—and reproduce one historical real-Rust bug with it.**

Before implementing a framework, manually construct the witness and establish that Crabtree/SyRust/current fuzz-driver synthesis cannot express or reach it under a comparable budget. If you cannot identify that specific expressive gap first, stop.

Also, Miri success cannot serve as evidence of soundness. Miri should be a witness executor: a failing execution is evidence under the pinned model/tool version; many successful executions are not a proof.

**This is the proposal I would stop.** Its remaining niche is too dependent on discovering a very particular missing client behavior after several strong lines of work have attacked essentially the same existential question.

1. migration units that preserve allocation responsibility — keep only after pivoting

Opinion: potentially interesting systems work, but the current “jointly choose representations and migration groups” framing is no longer sufficiently distinct.

PLDI 2026 &inator explicitly makes interface representation a global reasoning problem. Its abstract says interface translation produces Rust declarations for “structs and function signatures,” then optimizes for a correct, least-cost Rust interface. [DOI+1](https://doi.org/10.1145/3808270?utm_source=chatgpt.com) That is a direct objection to representation choice being the new ingredient.

Fortunately, its implementation has a useful boundary: its published limitations include “function pointers” among unsupported C features. [GitHub](https://github.com/PLaSSticity/refinator-impl?utm_source=chatgpt.com) Your callbacks are therefore not a cosmetic detail; they may be where the real problem is.

I would reformulate the question as:

**For partial migration with a frozen C ABI, which ownership-transfer-connected functions should cross the language boundary together when callbacks and allocator/deallocator obligations prevent ordinary safe interface inference?**

Do one library first, not two. Pick a library containing all three: transferred heap buffer, callback/function pointer, and asymmetric create/free ownership. Manually label allocation/transfer/destruction events. Compare perhaps three partitions under exactly the same translation and debugging budget: size/call-graph partition, ownership-transfer partition, expert manual partition.

Primary outcome: **cross-boundary ownership obligations**, not raw-pointer count. Count unsafe adapter sites, lifetime/cleanup obligations reviewers must reason about, wrapper machinery, and representation revisions forced later. Differential tests establish behavioral evidence, not equivalence.

If removing callbacks makes &inator able to formulate essentially the same representation problem, that should be treated as evidence that your novelty comes from **partial migration plus unsupported boundary mechanisms**, not “better representation choices.”

1. dependency permissions that change between releases — keep; run early

Opinion: lowest conceptual ambition of the four, but probably the cleanest inexpensive empirical question.

Cackle already kills enforcement novelty. It says each build-script sandbox is separately configured and explicitly warns that problematic APIs may appear only under configurations different from the one tested. [GitHub](https://github.com/cackle-rs/cackle)

More interestingly, cargo-sandbox exposes almost exactly the assumption worth measuring: its policy currently “uses just the package name” and says “we don't actually care about which version or the source of it,” while noting source is a TODO. [GitHub](https://github.com/madsmtm/cargo-sandbox) That gives you a concrete, falsifiable maintenance question rather than a generic supply-chain story.

The narrow experiment should be longitudinal:

**How often does the least privilege required for a successful build change across release × feature-set × target, and how much human review does preserving a package-name policy versus regenerating a configuration-specific policy require?**

Start with perhaps 8–12 deliberately selected crates covering -sys native builds, code generation, proc macros, and cross-compilation; two or three successive releases; two feature configurations; one native and one cross target. Linux alone is enough initially.

Distinguish three things rigorously: capabilities granted by policy, operations actually observed, and permissions demonstrated necessary by denial/retry. An observed syscall is not automatically “necessary.” Conversely, an unobserved malicious operation tells you nothing about whether a dormant dependency could perform it.

I would drop “without missing documented attack actions” as a primary success criterion unless you explicitly execute safe reproductions in a disposable environment. Otherwise the result overclaims from incomplete dynamic coverage.

The interesting result is a measurable frontier between **policy stability, privilege overgrant, build success, and reviewer work**. If ordinary Cackle configurations remain stable across your holdout releases/configurations with little unnecessary privilege, obey your stop condition.

Overall decision

I would allocate effort in this order:

1. **proposal 1:** first serious research probe; highest payoff if you find a cross-attempt/external-history failure missed by existing cancellation approaches
2. **proposal 4:** run early because it is cheap and can quickly produce either evidence or a justified stop
3. **proposal 3:** proceed only after rewriting it around frozen partial-migration boundaries, callbacks, and explicit ownership transfer; &inator makes the current wording vulnerable
4. **proposal 2:** stop; retain only a tiny discriminating experiment if you can name an unsupported adversarial caller construct before implementation

The common principle I would use is stronger than “existing tool does not check this.” Require a **failure oracle that comes from the system's independent contract**, plus one concrete execution that the strongest closest baseline fails to expose under equal resources. Proposals 1 and 4 naturally permit that. Proposal 3 can after narrowing. Proposal 2 currently starts from a generator idea and has to search for a remaining failure class, which is the wrong direction after Crabtree, SyRust, Rudra, RUXt, and recent Rust fuzz-driver work.
