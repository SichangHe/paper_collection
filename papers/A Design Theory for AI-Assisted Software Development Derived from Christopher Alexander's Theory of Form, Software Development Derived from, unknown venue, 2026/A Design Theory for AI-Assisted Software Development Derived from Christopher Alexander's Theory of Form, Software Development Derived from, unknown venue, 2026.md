Abstract
In AI-assisted software development, code generated by large language models (LLMs) cannot
be assumed to meet specified requirements. Conventional correctness-assurance measures
such as reviews, testing, design by contract, and static analysis remain applicable. Verification,
however, must keep pace with an unreliable generator. The open question is which of these
measures a sufficient harness needs and what role each plays. This paper proposes a design
theory for AI-assisted software development, derived from Christopher Alexander’s theory of
form rather than from prompting or agentic-programming techniques. It also provides a
methodology for applying the theory in practice.

The theory builds on Alexander’s view of design as achieving fit between a form, the software
under development, and its context, the demands that software must satisfy. On this account,
fit can be perceived only negatively, through the absence of identified misfits. We apply this
view to AI coding by first making the development organization’s tradition explicit, then deriving
the misfits from that tradition and from the problem’s classification. The harness then checks
the generated software against those misfits.

The theory models the LLM as a non-native vernacular builder: a builder trained on many
codebases but native to none, whose output tends to drift toward mainstream conventions
rather than the local tradition. For such a builder, the theory recreates in explicit machinery
what Alexander’s unselfconscious process supplied through tradition: adherence is enforced
rather than left to the builder’s judgment. From Alexander’s analysis, we derive obligations that
must be discharged for the absence of detected misfits to justify a stated claim of fit. We
discharge these obligations by engineering four pieces of machinery that Alexander’s
vernacular cultures did not need. First, the problem is made explicit through a representation
based on Jackson’s problem frames, and the solution-side tradition is made explicit as a four-
form pattern language. Second, a stack of deterministic detectors makes misfits manifest.
Third, a fix loop drives repair until the gates pass or the problem is escalated to a human. Fourth,
a human-gated legislative circuit governs changes to the representations and detectors
themselves. We call the resulting methodology Misfit-Governed Development (MGD). The
builder cannot change this machinery on its own authority. Building and maintaining such
machinery is the emerging practice of harness engineering.

The resulting process separates an autonomous inner loop, where the LLM iterates against the
gates, from a human outer loop, where specifications are judged against the world and revised
along with the tradition when they no longer fit. We call this the dual-loop process. The inner
loop checks three families of relations by machine: the program and the tests must each

conform to the specification, and the program must pass the tests. The outer loop carries the
one judgment the machinery cannot make on its own authority: whether the specification fits
the world. Together they form the S = P = T = W assurance model, where S stands for
specification, P for program, T for tests, and W for world; the equals sign is a mnemonic for
these relations rather than a claim of literal equality. We report empirical evidence from
building and rebuilding a Scrum system whose local tradition draws on domain-driven design,
event sourcing, Clean Architecture, and Command Query Responsibility Segregation. Its four
event-sourced aggregates are built from 64 problem-frame specifications and verified by
approximately 1,300 generated tests under two infrastructure profiles and by 28 blocking gates,
one of which applies 188 machine-checkable rules. This experience addresses the generativity
dimension of Alexander’s 1996 OOPSLA challenge to the software community. The moral
dimension cannot be discharged by a gate: whether the specification still fits the world requires
human judgment, which the theory locates in the outer loop as an institutional obligation rather
than a detection problem.

1. Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) can generate plausible code at a rate that makes exhaustive
human review increasingly costly. Worse, generated code can make an entire class of failures
unusually difficult to notice through conventional code review. A misunderstood requirement
sometimes leaves visible traces: the code may read awkwardly, violate local conventions, or
fail to compile. Generated code weakens the evidential value of those traces: it can express the
same misunderstanding as fluent implementation. It may compile and pass superficial
inspection while silently dropping a field, inverting a guard condition, or defaulting to
mainstream conventions learned during training. The practice known as vibe coding [1], in
which generated code is accepted on the strength of its apparent plausibility, leaves the
correctness question unanswered.

The methodology described in this paper takes the opposite approach. It treats the generator as
permanently unreliable. This is a trust policy, not a prediction about future model capability.
Prompt engineering and more capable models may improve the odds of success, but improved
odds do not by themselves turn an LLM into a trustworthy translator from intent to code. Better
odds are a property of the generator. They do not establish that any particular generated system
fits its context. Therefore, the methodology withdraws trust from the generator and places it in a
human-governed verification stack that surrounds the generator. Recent work has begun to
treat this surrounding machinery as an engineering object in its own right, cataloging the
harness’s runtime responsibilities [2] and evolving harnesses automatically against observed
failures [3]. The design problem then becomes the following: what must the verification stack
contain, and what theory can tell us when it is sufficient for a stated claim of fit?

Our answer returns to Christopher Alexander’s classical theory of form, set out in Notes on the
Synthesis of Form [4], which preceded the pattern-language work later embraced by software
engineering. There Alexander analyzed design as the achievement of fit between a form (the
thing we shape) and its context (the part of the world that places demands on it). He observed
that good fit can be perceived only negatively. Misfits, by contrast, can be identified individually,
enumerated, and detected. This observation has a direct implication for AI coding. To the extent
that the relevant misfits can be enumerated and mechanically detected, synthesizing the form
ceases to be a design problem and becomes a technical one. Fit is operationalized, relative to

                                                 2
the enumeration, as the absence of every enumerated misfit. Functional incorrectness is only
one class of misfit: a system can meet its functional requirements and still misfit its context by
violating architectural, contractual, or local-tradition demands. Synthesis then reduces to
eliminating those misfits in turn. The strength of the resulting fit claim depends on how
complete the enumeration is. In practice the enumeration is never finished, and not every
recognized misfit yields a mechanical detector. The methodology therefore treats the
enumeration itself as a governed, growing artifact: every escape either extends or corrects the
enumeration or receives an explicit ruling (Sections 4.4 and 5).

Alexander further distinguished the unselfconscious process of vernacular building cultures
from the self-conscious process of modern design. In the unselfconscious process, a builder
without a global model of the problem achieves fit by following tradition and correcting misfits
one at a time as reality makes them manifest. The self-conscious process cannot reliably
achieve fit because the interacting misfits are too complex to treat as a whole, while the
conceptual categories introduced to decompose them can distort the real misfit structure [4].

By the time Alexander addressed OOPSLA in 1996, software engineering had adopted his
patterns as an engineering vocabulary. Alexander questioned whether their generative and
moral substance had carried over [5]. He challenged the field to make software genuinely
generative, to aim at coherent wholes, and to make life better.

This paper takes up that challenge by developing a design theory in the established sense of the
term [6, 7]. The theory is prescriptive, grounded in kernel theories, and testable. This is not
another prompting technique or agentic workflow. The paper makes five contributions:

   1. A design theory for AI coding, operationalized through Misfit-Governed
      Development. We reframe AI code generation as Alexander’s form–context problem.
      Misfit enumeration becomes the basis of verification. Machine-checked fit is the
      absence of every misfit in the mechanized enumeration. The central question is
      therefore how complete the enumeration is, rather than how capable the generator is
      (Sections 2, 3, 4.2, 4.5, and 6; Table 1; Appendix A.1).
   2. The non-native vernacular builder. We model the generator as a craftsman trained
      across many traditions and native to none, whose deviations pull persistently toward
      mainstream idiom. This model and software’s silent misfits yield enforced tradition,
      manufactured manifestation, and severed backflow (Sections 3.5, 4.1, 4.4, and 4.5).
   3. The four-form pattern language. The language’s reader is a machine rather than a
      judicious professional. Every pattern node must exist in four coupled forms (doctrine,
      template, prohibition, gate), and the language’s grammar is enforced rather than
      followed. It is paired with a problem representation whose frame catalog extends
      Jackson’s. Each problem-frame type’s workflow is an ordered traversal of the solution-
      side pattern network (Section 3).
   4. The dual-loop process and the S = P = T = W assurance model. Building the
      machinery that surrounds the generator is a practice that has developed faster than its
      theory. We derive a theory for its verification core. The separation of machine-paced
      and human-paced feedback follows from the asymmetry between machine-priced and
      human-priced detection rather than from a design preference; the two loops are its
      realization. The resulting dual-loop process is the engineered analogue of Alexander’s
      self-correcting vernacular process, yielding the S = P = T = W assurance model. Here, S
      denotes specification, P program, T tests, and W world. The model comprises three
      families of machine-decidable relations enclosed by a fourth relation, S = W, that
      requires human judgment; the equals signs abbreviate these relations, and Appendix A
      gives their formal semantics (Sections 3.1, 3.5–3.7, and 4.2–4.3).


                                                 3
    5. A graded answer to Alexander’s OOPSLA challenge. The methodology operationalizes
       generativity for software pattern languages. The LLM can interpret a pattern language as
       written while being deterministically governed by it. Coherence is answered in the
       formal sense (the parts verifiably compose) and left open in the experiential sense
       (Alexander’s felt wholeness). The moral dimension, whether the built thing serves
       human life, enters the assurance model through the S = W relation, which no gate can
       discharge. It must therefore be answered by institutions rather than detectors (Section
       6).

These contributions form a single chain rather than five independent claims. The builder model
motivates the pattern language, the pattern language requires the harness that enforces it, and
the asymmetry between machine-priced and human-priced detection separates that harness’s
feedback into the two loops of Contribution 4. Together they constitute the design theory
introduced in Contribution 1 and ground its graded answer to Alexander’s challenge
(Contribution 5).

2. Background and the Derivation
2.1 Fit, misfit, and the negative definition of good design
Alexander’s Notes on the Synthesis of Form [4] frames every design problem as an ensemble: a
form to be shaped and a context that places demands on it. Its central observation is an
asymmetry of perception. Designers cannot perceive good fit positively. They can only detect
misfits (specific, enumerable failures of the form to meet a demand). Fit is therefore assessed
negatively, through the absence of identified misfits. We operationalize fit, relative to an
enumerated misfit list, as the absence of every misfit on that list (Appendix A). How complete
the list is becomes the central question. To the extent that the relevant misfits have been
enumerated and made decidable, the verdict on a candidate form becomes mechanical. What
lies outside that enumeration remains a matter for judgment. Alexander himself conceded that
the selection of misfit variables cannot be formalized, a concession that, as we show in
Sections 5 and 6 (see also Table 1, P8), predicts the residue of judgment that remains in our
methodology.

Design research later re-examined this negative formulation. Salustri [8] judges Notes “flawed,
but not flawed beyond use”: he keeps the misfit core, but because a representation that
records only unsatisfactory aspects can miss a repair’s effect on aspects that were previously
satisfactory, he extends it into a balance among interacting properties. We retain the negative
formulation for verification and instead make its scope explicit: fit is claimed only relative to the
enumerated misfits (Appendix A). Every repair re-runs the whole enumeration, so a disturbed
aspect that the enumeration covers appears as a new detected misfit (Section 3.6), while
misfits outside the enumeration remain the outer loop’s responsibility (Section 4.4).

2.2 The unselfconscious process and its enabling conditions
Alexander’s account of vernacular building cultures [4] describes a process that achieves fit
without any participant holding a global model. The builder follows tradition, a crystallized
memory of resolved misfits that includes taboos. Tradition also supplies viscosity: it resists

                                                  4
change, so each generation of builders starts near known-good form. Reality makes misfits
manifest (the roof leaks). Correction is then local and immediate. We emphasize three enabling
conditions usually left implicit:

   1. Manifestation is physical: failures convert themselves into sensory signals without
      engineered detectors.
   2. Perception is collocated: those who suffer a misfit, those who notice it, and those who
      can repair it live with the building, often as one and the same person, so detection is a
      free by-product of dwelling.
   3. Interpretation is cultural: tradition has pre-decided what counts as a misfit. The same
      draft is a defect in one building culture and desirable ventilation in another.

Under these conditions the process is homeostatic [4]. Good fit is maintained because rapid
local correction closes the feedback loop between reality and form, and because the
vernacular’s misfit network decomposes into weakly coupled subsystems, so that repairs
rarely propagate.

When Alexander later confronted a setting where these conditions fail (a modern university
campus), he did not pretend they still held. He engineered a prosthetic mechanism. The Oregon
Experiment’s diagnosis principle [9] institutionalizes misfit perception. A periodic survey of the
campus, scored against the pattern language, feeds a repair budget. This precedent matters
because none of these conditions can be assumed to hold for the non-native software builder
of this paper. Each will have to be engineered back (Section 3).

2.3 The arc to The Timeless Way and the OOPSLA challenge
The Oregon prosthetic repaired one institution’s failing process from the outside. Alexander’s
larger project was to rebuild tradition itself. Between Notes and The Timeless Way of Building
[10], his position matured into what we read as a three-stage arc. The shared, implicit
languages of vernacular cultures have broken down. Self-conscious design fails by substituting
the designer’s arbitrary categories for the real misfit structure. The remedy is to reconstruct
tradition self-consciously through an explicit pattern language whose patterns state the forces
they resolve [11], with the ultimate aim of re-internalization. The final chapter of The Timeless
Way treats the explicit language as scaffolding: the master builder “no longer needs the
language” and responds directly to the forces themselves [10]. The image is Alexander’s own:
the part of the book that presents the pattern language is titled “The Gate,” and the way is not
complete “until we leave the gate behind” [10]. In this account, the explicit language is a gate
that the builder passes through and eventually leaves behind, a passage toward mastery rather
than a permanent checkpoint. We call this hoped-for endpoint, in which the builder acts from
the tradition without consulting the language, Alexander’s forgetting.

At OOPSLA 1996 [5], Alexander told the software patterns community that it had taken his
vocabulary, and he questioned whether three central concerns had come with it: the moral
dimension, coherence, and generativity. The moral dimension asks whether patterns make life
better, coherence whether the built result hangs together as a single whole, and generativity
whether a language generates coherent wholes the way a genome generates an organism
rather than merely serving as a catalog of parts. He closed by asking whether software (a field
with more practitioners and more leverage than architecture) would use its computational
power to build genuinely generative processes.

2.4 Problem frames
While Alexander supplies the theory of fit, Jackson supplies the vocabulary that we use to state
the problem. Michael Jackson’s problem frames [12] are, in our reading, the software analogue
of Alexander’s ensemble. The problem is in the world; the machine is the solution. Analysis
consists of drawing the boundary between them and classifying the problem into recurring
frame types, each carrying a known set of frame concerns. In Alexandrian terms, that set
supplies a pre-enumerated core of the misfit list whose cost is amortized across every problem
of that class. This amortization makes broad enumeration operational for the recurring part of
the context. No project can enumerate its context from scratch, but a project that classifies its
problems into frames inherits a reusable starting structure for the enumeration. The project
must still enumerate its own domain-specific misfits on top of that structure (Section 3.2).

2.5 From propositions to obligations: the derivation, stated explicitly
The title of this paper claims that our theory is derived from Alexander’s theory of form, not
merely inspired by it, and this claim should be supported explicitly. Table 1 states the mapping.
Each row names a proposition of the classical theory (stated explicitly by Alexander, in P7 and
P9 drawing on his later work [9, 10], or reconstructed from the mechanisms he describes), the
engineering obligation it imposes once the builder is the non-native LLM of Section 4.1, and the
mechanism that discharges it. None of the entries is arbitrary. The derivation fixes the
obligations but not the mechanisms. Each obligation follows from the proposition in the left-
hand column under the engineering setting considered here, together with, where relevant, the
premise that the builder is a non-native LLM. The mechanisms in the right-hand column are
engineering choices. Each is one way of discharging its obligation but not the only way. The
rows are presented separately for exposition, but they are not claimed to be logically
independent: an obligation may draw on its proposition together with the builder premise and
with obligations established by earlier rows. Alexander’s propositions, the traditions the
methodology draws on, and the S = P = T = W model were all in hand before the harness was
built, and they guided its construction. The derivation, however, does not rest on that history. It
stands or falls on whether each obligation follows from its stated proposition together with the
builder premise and any prior obligations on which the row depends.

The ten rows are not claimed to be exhaustive. A proposition earns a row by one test: it must
yield an engineering obligation once the builder premise changes. A proposition that yields no
new obligation under that change is omitted. The best-known operational content of Notes, its
formal procedure for decomposing misfit variables into hierarchical subsets, does not qualify
for a row. It is a mechanism rather than a proposition, and its structural role is filled here by
domain-driven decomposition (P5). This exclusion is also consistent with Alexander’s own later
assessment. In the preface to the paperback edition [4], he set the mathematics aside while
retaining the account of fit and misfit on which the derivation builds.

Like the misfit list itself, the table remains open, but at a different level. An escape (a defect that
the gates missed) is first answered within the existing rows: a new detector, a repaired one, or a
reconciled proxy discharges the obligations already stated (Section 4.4; exercised in Section 5).
Only an escape that exposes an engineering obligation not derivable from any existing row calls
for a new row. Appendix A states the underlying misfit calculus formally.

The derivation claims unity, not exclusivity. Some obligations can also be justified by other
software engineering theories. For example, the obligation that fit must rest on the verdict of
detectors (P1) and the obligation to manufacture manifestation where misfits do not reveal
themselves (P2) are consistent with long-standing verification practice, and the cadence
asymmetry of P7 is consistent with iterative development. The claim is not that Alexander alone

                                                  6
can justify these obligations. The claim is that all ten arise from a single account of fit rather
than being assembled from unrelated engineering principles. Three of them also arrive with a
different status from their nearest counterparts in current practice. Practice knows change
control and verification independence as precautions. Here, the severed backflow between
builder and tradition (P6), the permanence of the scaffold (P9), and the obligation to place every
governing representation, not only the tests, under verification (P10) follow as consequences of
the builder premise, from Alexander’s analyses of vernacular culture, of internalization, and of
representations. Sections 4.4, 4.5, and 4.2 develop them at length, and Observation 2 exercises
P10 in operation. Table 1 should therefore be read as a derivation from one kernel theory, not as
a claim that it is the only possible derivation. The division runs as follows. Five of the ten (P1, P2,
P4, P5, P7) express requirements that do not depend specifically on the builder being an LLM,
and a reader who already practices verification will recognize them. Four (P3, P6, P9, P10) take
on their present force because the builder is an LLM: it cannot be shown to have internalized
the local tradition, it defaults to the mainstream, and its access to context is mediated through
representations. P8 sits between the two groups. The residue of judgment exists for any builder,
but once the builder is mechanized, no one can establish that it carries that residue, so the
obligation to place the residue outside the autonomous inner loop is specific to this setting. We
state the division rather than leave it implicit, because the contribution is the derivation of all
ten from one account of fit, not a claim that each is new.

3. The Methodology
We name the methodology Misfit-Governed Development (MGD): candidate forms are governed
by the explicit enumeration, detection, and repair of misfits between the form and its context.

                                                              9
The builder in this paper is a generative actor with a non-native LLM at its core (Section 4.1), and
a candidate is admitted only when the harness detects no enumerated misfit.

In this paper, we distinguish the law from the verification harness (hereafter, the harness). The
law consists of the design-time artifacts of Figure 1, namely, the problem representation and
the solution-side language, together with the misfit list. Anything that carries normative
authority over what the builder produces, but cannot render a verdict, belongs to the law. In
practice, the law tells the builder what system to produce and how to produce it in the project’s
local tradition.

The harness is the run-time machinery that enforces the law, namely, gates, fix loops,
reconciliation checks, and their own verification. Anything that can render a verdict or halt
progress belongs to the harness. In practice, the harness determines whether the generated
artifacts conform to the law. The other run-time machinery an agent needs, such as managing
its context or routing its tool calls, is outside our scope. Section 7 relates this focus to the wider
harness-engineering literature.

The boundary between the law and the harness is defined by role rather than by execution time.
A gate deliberately connects the two sides: its governing rule is authored at design time and
belongs to the law — as a pattern’s fourth form, or as a specification-side rule (Section 3.4) —
while the executable checker that evaluates the rule renders verdicts at run time and belongs to
the harness. Every pattern includes its own checker by methodological requirement; rule and
checker are authored together so that the executable verdict stays coupled to its governing rule.

At run time, the law is also present, carried into the builder’s prompts as guidance. Guidance
can inform the builder, but it cannot render a verdict or halt progress. The same distinction
applies to the generation workflow of Section 3.3. The prescribed traversal order guides the
builder and therefore belongs to the law. The harness does not drive the traversal. Instead, it
refuses to conclude one while any gate fails or any step remains unrecorded. It returns each
failing verdict to the builder.

Building and maintaining the run-time machinery around an agent has recently come to be
known as harness engineering [2, 3]. We adopt the term for the verification harness studied
here. The novelty is not the term itself but a derived account of what a sufficient verification
harness must contain and why (Section 2.5). The governing principle is that verification does not
stop at the generated code. The law itself, and the machinery that enforces it, must also be
verified — for internal integrity and fidelity to its sources; its fit with the world remains the outer
loop’s judgment (Section 4.2). This does not regress without end: the chain of checks
terminates at a mechanically consultable primary where one exists, or at the human ruling that
authorizes the proxy (Section 4.2; Appendix A).

Table 1’s derivation can now be distilled into seven governing principles. Where Table 1
preserves the derivation of the obligations, these principles turn their governing consequences
into rules for operating the methodology. Each is derived in the sections indicated below, and
none is merely advisory. Every one is realized as an enforceable property of the reference
implementation, through the gate stack, the structure of the dual-loop harness, or the human-
gated legislative circuit (Sections 3.7 and 4.4).

    1. Trust the stack, not the generator. The generator’s fluency has no standing as
       evidence of correctness (P1; §3.5). A stronger generator improves the odds that a
       candidate satisfies the harness, but the harness’s verdict still determines whether the
       candidate is accepted. A verdict counts only if the responsible detectors actually ran
       and their liveness is verified: a skipped gate has not passed (P2; §3.4–3.5, §5). The
       representations on which that verdict depends are themselves verified and reconciled

                                                  10
        with their sources (P10; §4.2, §5). And a history of passing verdicts gives no grounds for
        retiring enforcement: observed compliance cannot establish that the builder has
        internalized the tradition (P9; §4.5).
   2.   Enforcement over guidance. Prompt-carried guidance cannot be trusted to carry
        compliance. Only gates are authoritative (P1, P3; §4.1, §5).
   3.   In this methodology, a pattern exists only in four forms: doctrine, template,
        prohibition, and gate. A pattern lacking any of the four is treated as not existing (P3;
        §3.4).
   4.   A false positive is never fixed in the generated artifacts. If the gate is wrong, fix the
        gate. If the gate is right but the law behind it is wrong, revise the law through the
        legislature of Principle 6. Never modify the generated artifacts simply to make a broken
        check pass. Otherwise, the error in the gate or in the law behind it propagates into the
        generated artifacts (P6, P10; §3.7, §4.4).
   5.   Detection cost sets the affordable feedback cadence. Machine-priced detection
        affords a continuous inner loop. Human-priced detection affords only a budgeted outer
        loop (P7; §4.3). The separation of cadences is a consequence of this asymmetry rather
        than a design preference; the dual-loop architecture realizes it.
   6.   The builder may not amend the law. Repairs belong in the generated artifacts, never in
        the tradition. Amendments pass through the human legislature (P6; §3.7, §4.4). This
        does not freeze the law. The model may draft and even execute an amendment. It may
        never approve one.
   7.   Machines verify S = P = T; humans judge S = W. The first three relations are
        mechanized so that human attention can be devoted to the fourth (P8; §4.2, §6). S = W is
        the outer loop’s irreducibly human relation, not its only responsibility: the same loop
        also governs the law and the harness on which the machine verdicts depend (Principle
        6).

3.1 The dual-loop model
Figure 2 presents a dual-loop conceptual model of AI-assisted software development. A human
designs the law, a representation of both the problem and the local tradition, and iteratively
amends it as new misfits emerge during verification and use. An LLM performs a non-
deterministic transformation of this representation into candidate code. The candidate then
enters an inner loop. The loop runs inside an autonomous agent zone, iterating the candidate
against deterministic verification by tests, contracts, and static checks. A human may help (say,
approving an operation or answering a question about the specification) but never judges the
candidate. Within this loop the specification is provisionally assumed correct. A failed verdict
provisionally blames the candidate, never the specification, and triggers automatic retry.

The verified solution is executed by a machine (the runtime and executable contracts),
producing observable behavior. An outer loop returns observations of that behavior to the
human, who revises the governing representations. At a slower cadence, the same loop also
returns observations of the system’s lived consequences. When failure persists across
repeated inner-loop retries, the loop stops blaming successive candidates and escalates to the
outer loop for diagnosis.

The revised artifact is not always a specification. Sometimes the tradition itself must be revised.
An escape, a defect that slipped past every gate, triggers diagnosis of the governing chain: if the
law already represented the violated expectation, the escape reveals a detection fault — a
detector missing, ineffective, unreachable, or reading a stale proxy; if it did not, it reveals an
omission in the law or a demand newly discovered in the world (Section 4.4). A false positive, a
gate flagging code that should not have been flagged, reveals a fault in the gate when the

                                                11
flagged code conforms to the law; when the gate is right but the law behind it is wrong, the fault
lies in the law (Principle 4). In both cases the remedy lies outside the generated artifacts and
flows through the legislative circuit of Section 3.7. Either way, revision happens at design time,
never inside the inner loop. Every verdict is rendered against expectations, and the
expectations live in the governing representations, specifically in the specifications and the
misfit list. Because humans author these representations, verification can be performed
deterministically by machines while accountability remains with humans.

3.2 The problem representation: problem-frame specifications
The problem side is written in a structured specification language organized by frame type.
Each use case, once classified under a frame type, is specified by five kinds of artifact, stored in
its own directory at {project}/{bounded-context}/aggregates/{aggregate}/{frame-
type}/{use-case}/:

   1. frame.yaml, which names the frame type, the problem-world facts, and the frame
      concerns.
   2. Requirement files, which carry the description, the rationale, and the acceptance
      criteria that verify them.
   3. A machine specification, which defines the machine’s responsibilities, the use case
      with its input and output, and the controller that exposes it as an endpoint.
   4. A domain specification, which is frame-type-specific. For state-mutating frames it
      carries the aggregate part with its attributes, domain events, invariants, and Design-by-
      Contract clauses [13]. For the others it carries the read model, the transformation’s
      input/output contract, or the trigger wiring.
   5. acceptance.yaml, which states the scenarios in Given–When–Then form, with full
      traceability to the requirements.

These artifacts are written per use case, not all in advance. A use case can be analyzed,
specified, and generated on its own, and its domain specification carries only the part of the
aggregate that the use case touches, which is why that artifact is named the aggregate part. The


                                                                   12
specification therefore grows piece by piece with the system, in Alexander’s manner of
piecemeal growth [9], rather than being completed up front.

Seven frame types are currently distinguished. Five are inherited from Jackson’s catalog [12]:
commanded behavior (CBF), simple workpieces (SWF), required behavior (RBF), information
display (IDF), and transformation (TF). The sixth, reactive invocation (RIF), is an addition of ours
in which a triggering event or schedule invokes a further operation, in this architecture most
commonly to maintain eventual consistency between aggregates (Section 3.3). The seventh,
the connection frame (CF), is an extension of ours that elevates the role played by connection
domains in Jackson’s framework [12] into a frame type of its own. It specifies the connection
between separated processes, which this architecture realizes as infrastructure relays carrying
events between them, and it lies outside the application tree and therefore outside the
empirical counts of Section 5.

The specification language itself has machine-checked syntax and partially machine-checked
semantics. JSON Schemas constrain the core file types. A 39-rule specification linter enforces
format, cross-file traceability, structural well-formedness, and cross-frame value-object
consistency. A controlled semantics vocabulary of declarative tags (e.g., value_immutable,
identity, soft_delete_flag) maps each tag to mandatory code-generation consequences
(no setter generated, invariant clause injected). The problem representation itself, namely, the
frame catalog, the schemas, the linter rules, and the semantics vocabulary, is amendable only
through the human-gated legislative circuit of Section 3.7.

The tree above the frames is organized by domain-driven design’s decomposition units
(bounded contexts and, within them, aggregates [14]). As a result, the problem-side
decomposition and the solution-side packaging use the same units. An aggregate is therefore
both a unit of specification and a unit of generated code. This organization belongs to the
representation, not to the frame types. The problem representation is architecture-dependent.
The frame types themselves classify the problem and are architecture-neutral. The
representation built on them expresses the stakeholder demands in the vocabulary that the
chosen solution family requires.

Before the specifications are written, an Event Storming workshop [15] brings domain experts
and developers together to model the business flow as a timeline of domain events. From that
timeline, commands, policies, read models, aggregates, and bounded contexts are identified,
and a class diagram of the domain is drawn. The frame types are deliberately aligned with Event
Storming’s grammar; the mapping that follows is our classification scheme, not part of
Jackson’s catalog. Task-based commands, those that ask the system to carry out a business
activity, become commanded behavior frames. Commands that simply establish a state
become simple workpieces frames. Read models become information display frames. Policies
(“whenever this event occurs, do that”) are classified by their reaction. A policy that invokes a
command becomes a reactive invocation frame; one that maintains a read model becomes a
transformation frame. A rule that obliges the system to act on its own, when a condition holds
rather than when a user commands, becomes a required behavior frame. The aggregates that
organize the specification tree are discovered on the Event Storming wall.

Once a unit is classified under a frame type, it inherits that type’s frame concerns, namely, its
pre-enumerated misfit list (Section 2.4). Event Storming is thus the methodology’s structured
first contact with the primary problem context (Section 4.2). The problem-side misfits that the
frames later formalize have two sources: the generic concerns come with the frame type, and
the domain-specific demands that instantiate them originate with the domain experts, although
the specification process may reveal omissions that require further elicitation. Figure 3 shows a
classified slice of such a wall.

3.3 The solution-side language: a pattern network
The solution-side language is a network of patterns that holds the chosen architecture fixed. It
was drawn before any prompt, gate, or workflow existed. The network does not prescribe every
form the builder may produce. Figures 4–8 show only the architecturally significant part of the
network, not the complete language. Design- and implementation-level patterns are introduced
later, in the templates and rules attached to each node of the pattern network (Section 3.4).
Those templates and rules incorporate cataloged design patterns (e.g., [14, 16, 17, 18, 19])
together with the team’s local coding conventions.

The network itself is an engineering choice in the sense of Section 2.5, not part of the theory.
Alexander expected this diversity, for every living culture has its own pattern language [11]. This
network is our team’s architectural culture made explicit. The culture itself is not new. It has
accumulated since 2018 through the continuous development of ezKanban, a Kanban system
we built with domain-driven design, Clean Architecture, event sourcing, Command Query
Responsibility Segregation (CQRS), and design by contract [20].

The network is also selective. It includes a pattern only when the team constrains how it is used.
The builder knows many more from training. If the team does not constrain a pattern, the
network simply leaves it out. The builder stays free to use it, within the fixed architecture. This
does not freeze the architecture. The team may still change the network through the legislative
circuit of Section 3.7, and because every pattern exists in four coupled forms, changing a
pattern also means changing its gate (Section 3.4). The derivation obliges only that some such
language exist and be enforced (Table 1, P3); which architectural family it encodes is revisited
in Section 8.

This section presents the macro-structure and its rules, and then unfolds each layer into its
own pattern map. Finally, it explains the locality this structure supplies and the order in which
workflows traverse it.

Macro-structure and rules. The macro-structure is Clean Architecture [21], whose principles
we operationalize in the pattern language as three named rules (Figure 4):

    1. the Layering Rule: every artifact lives in one of four layers, Entities, Use Cases, Interface
       Adapters, and an IO layer (our name for Clean Architecture’s Frameworks and Drivers).



                                                 14
   2. the Dependency Rule: source dependencies point inward, from the IO layer toward the
      Entities layer.
   3. the Cross-Boundary Rule: layer crossings pass through ports, and domain objects
      themselves never cross. Inside the Use Cases layer, they are converted into a data
      transfer object toward the adapters or a persistent object toward storage. Incoming
      data structures are converted back into domain objects.

In the implementation, the Spring Boot entry class belongs to the IO layer’s Main component. It
sits in the project’s root package so that Spring Boot’s default component scan includes all
packages below it. All three rules are machine-checked (Section 3.5). The layering is enforced
by the structural gates, which pin each generated artifact’s package. The dependency and
cross-boundary rules are checked by architecture tests.

Locality. Beyond vocabulary, these boundaries supply the locality that a homeostatic process
requires. Bounded contexts partition the misfit network into weakly coupled subsystems, and
aggregates draw the boundaries within which repair is intended to be local. Domain events are
the deliberately weak coupling between subsystems. Consistency is transactional inside an
aggregate and eventual between aggregates, restored by reactive invocation frames that
consume one aggregate’s events and invoke operations on another [19].

Clean Architecture’s structural rules add the vertical counterpart to this horizontal
decomposition. Dependencies point only inward, and layers meet only at ports. These
boundaries constrain how far a repair’s effects can propagate, although they cannot force the
builder to keep its edits inside them; the regression guard of Section 3.6 covers that residual
risk. Neighboring layers touch the repaired aggregate only through its ports, and neighboring
aggregates hold only its identifiers. In practice, this lets different aggregates be generated and
repaired in parallel, while the methodology serializes work within one aggregate to preserve a
stable repair context. The identifier value objects that cross aggregate boundaries are
generated from a standard form and skipped if they already exist, so parallel runs do not collide
on them. Locality is necessary but not sufficient for homeostasis (Section 4.3 completes the
argument).

The authoring sequence and its traversal. The pattern language was drawn first as a network.
Each node in use then received its doctrine, template, prohibitions, and gate (Section 3.4’s four
coupled forms are per-node obligations). Finally, each problem-frame type received a workflow
that is an ordered traversal of a subgraph of this network. The traversal sets the order in which
nodes are generated, not the runtime call flow. For example, a commanded behavior frame
unfolds Entity-first: the Aggregate with its Domain Events, then the Command use case with its
ports, then the repository and outbox adapters, and then the controller. An information display
frame unfolds from the read model through its Projection to the Query use case and its
controller. A reactive invocation frame wires an existing trigger event to a generated Reactor

                                                               19
and its target operation. Every traversal ends in the IO layer, where a configuration class in the
aggregate’s own package wires the new parts into the running system (Figure 8). The
composition root discovers that class through component scan, so parallel traversals never
touch a shared file.

Drawing the language before writing any prompt, and enforcing it through each pattern’s gate,
gives the generated system its formal morphological coherence; Section 6 separates this
formal sense from Alexander’s felt wholeness. Individual prompts never negotiate their mutual
fit at generation time, because their intended compatibility was specified once, at the level of
the language.

3.4 The four-form pattern language
The language’s reader is an unreliable generator rather than a judicious professional. To govern
that reader, the methodology requires each pattern in the network to be realized in all four
coupled forms before the builder may generate from it. Together, these forms define what we
call a four-form pattern language:

   1. Doctrine: prose stating when the pattern applies and why.
   2. Template: the positive form, namely, code skeletons precise to the import path. The
      builder fills in the domain content. Everything else is supplied by the template.
   3. Prohibition: the negative constraints, namely, what must not appear and what must not
      be absent (95 always-active rules at the time of writing). A prohibition typically bans the
      mainstream idiom that the pattern replaces. A required construct is expressed as a
      prohibition on its absence.
   4. Gate: a deterministic checker, with a suite of good/bad fixtures showing that the
      checker still detects what it claims to detect, and real invocations showing that it still
      reaches what it claims to scan.

The four forms divide the labor rather than duplicate it. Doctrine explains when and why a
pattern applies. The template provides its positive form. The prohibitions encode the local
departures from the builder’s learned defaults and serve as the repair anchor for reported
violations. The gate alone renders the authoritative verdict. Like every mechanism in Table 1,
the four-form language is one way of discharging its obligation (P3), not the only way (Section
2.5).

A pattern that exists only as prose is treated as not existing, because prose guidance alone
does not reliably constrain the builder (Section 5); several drawn nodes of Section 3.3 remain
reserved vocabulary for future growth, not yet existing in this sense.

Not every gate is the fourth form of a pattern. The pattern network is the solution-side
representation (Section 3.3), and the four forms govern it. The gate stack also enforces the
problem side (Section 3.2). A rule may require that the receiving service read every input field
the specification declares, so that no input is silently dropped. Such a rule asks whether the
code discharges an obligation the specification states, rather than whether a construct has the
right shape, and the authority it cites is the specification schema rather than any pattern.

We keep these rules outside the pattern language rather than folding them into it. Both the
pattern network and the specifications are proxy contexts in the sense of Section 4.2, but they
proxy different things. The pattern network carries how the local tradition builds. The
specifications carry what this particular system must do. A gate that checks the second
belongs to the S = P or S = T relation of the assurance model (Section 4.2; Appendix A.2), not to



                                                20
the language. The harness runs both kinds in one stack, but that is an implementation
convenience. Treating every gate as a pattern’s fourth form would blur what a pattern is.

The language also keeps its own rules consistent. The same rule often appears in several
places, including a pattern’s doctrine, the guidance the builder reads, and the gate’s rule
catalog. Different consumers require different representations, so the language restates each
rule where it is consumed.

To prevent these copies from drifting apart, each rule has one designated original, recorded in a
single-source-of-authority registry. The other occurrences restate the rule in full rather than
merely pointing to it, because the builder must see the rule during generation; machine-
readable links tie each restatement to its original, and consistency checks fail until a diverged
copy follows its original. At the start of every session, before the builder generates anything,
self-consistency checks recompute facts asserted in more than one place and fail when two
sites disagree. Retired doctrine is governed the same way: superseded wording can reappear as
guidance, so a doctrine anti-pattern registry records it and a scanner flags it wherever it
resurfaces, in effect automating the forgetting of retired doctrine. Deletion happens once;
forgetting must be enforced continuously. All of this maintenance belongs to the outer loop
(Figure 2): the checks detect mechanically, but every amendment to the language is approved
by a human.

3.5 The gate stack: manufactured manifestation
At the time of writing, generation passes through 28 blocking gates of five kinds:

   1. a specification-quality gate: the linter of Section 3.2.
   2. a dual-profile test gate: every generated test suite, together with the permanent
      architecture tests of Section 3.3, must pass under both an in-memory and a full
      outbox/PostgreSQL infrastructure profile.
   3. eighteen per-use-case structural gates: for each use case, they check that the
      generated code and tests stay structurally faithful to its specification.
   4. seven aggregate-wide closeout gates covering spec-boundary, deliverable-manifest,
      encapsulation, and cross-frame consistency checks. Cross-aggregate checks do not
      form a kind of their own. Distributed across the dual-profile, structural, and closeout
      kinds, they cover globally unique endpoints, inter-aggregate import isolation, and the
      correct use of value objects and identifiers that belong to another aggregate.
   5. a deterministic-review gate applying 188 machine-checkable rules. For example, one
      rule forbids Instant.now() in domain code, so that time always comes from an
      injected provider and tests stay deterministic.

Two further gates sit outside the blocking count: advisory gates whose warnings (over-
specification and unnormalized optional inputs) surface but never block. One further
mechanism is not a gate at all: an optional multi-model semantic review, in which three LLMs
from different vendors review the generated code against the specification and an arbiter LLM
then resolves their disputed findings.

The gates compare the code against two different sets of expectations. The structural gates
compare it with the problem-frame specifications for each use case, while the closeout gates
compare it with the same specifications across the whole aggregate. The 188 deterministic-
review rules mostly compare it with the solution side: framework contracts, architectural
doctrine, and platform quirks, which are the same for every use case. The split mirrors the two
languages of Sections 3.2 and 3.3 (Section 4.2 develops the point).


                                                21
The eighteen structural gates fall into three broad families. Each family reads a different part of
the specification and demands the matching elements in the generated code and tests.
Coverage gates check that every specified scenario not deferred by the specification, and every
contract clause, has a corresponding test, that no test cites a scenario the specification does
not declare, and that each test asserts the kind of outcome its scenario declares and mentions
the states and quoted values the scenario names. Field gates verify field-level agreement
across representations. A field declared in the specification must reappear, correctly named
and typed, throughout its journey. Depending on the field, the journey spans value objects,
entities, events, mappers, persistent objects, and data transfer objects (DTOs). The field must
be genuinely carried at every hop. The converse check, that no field is realized without being
declared, is blocking for domain-event records, whose fields enter the permanent event log,
and advisory elsewhere. A companion rule in the deterministic-review gate blocks the same
kind of addition on the aggregate root: a public behavior method, or an event such a method
applies directly, that the specification does not declare. Controller gates extend the same
discipline to the HTTP boundary. They check request DTOs, endpoint semantics, error handling,
and controller-test completeness. A field that vanishes mid-chain (an input silently dropped by
a service, a derived value lost in reconstruction) is invisible to any single-layer check, because
every layer remains locally well-formed. It is detected only by checking the chain hop by hop,
field by field.

Every gate is deterministic. The determinism is essential, not incidental. A gate is an ordinary
program, a parser, a pattern matcher, or a test run. No language model sits in any gate’s verdict
path. The inner loop has no human in it to arbitrate, so a gate must return the same verdict on
the same artifact every time, given the same pinned inputs and environment (Section 3.6). A
detector that sometimes flags an artifact and sometimes passes it forces the judgment call that
the zone was designed to avoid (P8). A repair loop cannot reliably converge when the same
artifact receives different verdicts. Each fix round responds to a different verdict, and the
violation count can no longer show whether the repair is making progress. The vernacular’s
physics was deterministic in this sense: the roof leaks every time it rains.

The optional multi-model review is non-deterministic by design. The harness’s deterministic
predicates cannot decide whether the generated code faithfully reflects the specification’s
intent beyond what they already check (Appendix A). The review therefore estimates this
semantic faithfulness from the assessments of three LLMs from different vendors rather than
computing it directly. The review remained advisory throughout the study and accumulated
little operational history, so this paper reports it as part of the design, not as evaluated evidence.

3.6 The fix loop: mechanized vernacular repair
Gate failures do not end the process but enter a three-layer fix loop. The layers handle test
failures (the dual-profile suite), structural violations (batched across the structural gates), and
pattern violations (the deterministic-review rules). A repair round batches violations within one
layer only, and each layer has its own repair instructions. The optional multi-model review stays
outside the loop because a non-deterministic verdict cannot anchor convergent repair (Section
3.5). Its findings go to human adjudication instead. Five properties distinguish this fix loop from
a naive retry loop:

    1. Batching: re-verification is cheap because it only runs ordinary programs, while repair is
       expensive because it asks the builder to rewrite the code. Each round therefore collects
       every violation the layer reports and fixes them together. A dozen violations cost one
       repair round rather than a dozen, and violations that share a root cause are fixed as one.
       The vernacular process offered no comparable economy. Misfits revealed themselves
       through continued use of the building and were repaired as they appeared.

                                                 22
   2. Tradition-anchored repair: violations reach the builder not as bare verdicts but with the
      offending code, the violated rule, and a pointer into the tradition. A gate failure thus
      identifies the violated pattern and re-exposes its prescribed form as the repair target.
      Before fixing, the builder is instructed to re-read the authoritative pattern document for
      each rule it violated. Repair is thus guided by the tradition rather than by the model’s
      learned defaults. This matters because the risk of drifting back to mainstream code is
      highest during repair. By the time repair begins, the patterns read at generation time
      may no longer be present in the context window, and an unanchored model falls back
      on mainstream habit. The re-read instruction is guidance, not enforcement. A repair
      that ignores it and drifts back to the mainstream simply fails the gates again on the next
      round.
   3. Regression guarding: every repair round re-runs prior gates, because an unreliable
      builder’s fixes are not guaranteed to be local.
   4. Stall detection: if the violation count fails to decrease for three consecutive rounds, the
      loop halts and escalates to the human. This is an explicit valve. A problem that
      mechanical repair cannot resolve is handed upward as a design question. In
      Alexander’s terms, mechanical repair below the valve plays the unselfconscious role,
      and design judgment above it is self-conscious (Sections 2.2 and 2.3). The same
      escalation can happen before generation begins. If the builder cannot interpret the
      specification, the process halts and raises the question to the human rather than
      answering it by guesswork. The vernacular process had no corresponding valve. A repair
      that failed remained a problem for the culture itself rather than being escalated to a
      separate design authority.
   5. Context freezing: the context against which the generated artifact is judged remains
      fixed throughout a fix-loop window. Inputs under the workflow’s control, such as the
      specification and the framework version, are pinned when the loop begins. The tradition
      and the verification machinery cannot change from inside the loop and evolve only
      through the separate human-gated legislative circuit (Sections 3.7 and 4.4). This stable
      context allows Alexander’s one-at-a-time process of correction to converge (P4). Fixes
      can land one at a time only if the conditions against which they are judged are not
      changing beneath them. Stability makes convergence possible, not guaranteed; the
      stall valve of property 4 covers the difference.

Together, the five properties mean that no single repair round has to fix everything correctly. A
round needs only to reduce the violation count and to avoid breaking what already passed. The
gates check both conditions cheaply. The loop therefore tolerates imperfect rounds but
escalates when progress stalls.

3.7 What the builder may not touch
In every vernacular culture, repair experience flowed back into the tradition. What the
craftsman learned fixing the roof passed slowly, over generations, into that tradition. This
channel is deliberately severed. The LLM fixes the generated artifact at hand. It is never
permitted to modify templates, rules, or gates on its own authority.

The prohibition is enforced rather than requested (Principle 2, enforcement over guidance). The
builder cannot write to gate verdicts or workflow state. A guard rejects any attempt to do so.
Otherwise, the builder could simply rewrite the evidence that says whether its work passed.
Tradition files are protected differently. The builder can physically edit them, but such an edit
has no legislative standing: consistency checks detect the change and flag any dependent
document that no longer agrees. The same checks run again at the start of every session,
together with the doctrine-registry checks. An unauthorized change therefore cannot become

                                                23
part of the tradition on the builder’s authority alone: divergent copies are flagged, and even a
consistent rewrite acquires no legislative standing until a human accepts it. If this boundary
depended only on telling the builder not to cross it, it would be guidance, not enforcement.

The tradition can still change, but only through the separate, human-gated legislative circuit.
Changes come from three sources: defect post-mortems that must examine what the gates
missed (Section 4.4), improvements noticed during generation and saved for later batch review,
and audits of the verification artifacts themselves (Section 5). The model may make the edits,
but a human decides whether to accept them.

The same circuit handles errors in the gates themselves. If a gate rejects code that actually
conforms, the gate is fixed. The code is not changed just to make a broken gate pass. Section
4.4 explains why keeping this boundary is necessary, not just cautious.


4. Theoretical Analysis
This section develops the theory as a chain, each subsection answering the question the
previous one leaves open: what the builder is (4.1), what it is measured against (4.2), how often
the measurement can be afforded (4.3), who may amend the standard (4.4), and whether the
scaffold can ever come down (4.5).

4.1 The non-native vernacular builder
The vernacular builder’s reliability came from enculturation in a single internalized tradition.
Individual variations could be poor, but visible misfits were corrected before they became
established in the tradition. In an AI coding system, the builder is the generative actor that
produces and repairs the software, not the language model in isolation; in our implementation,
it comprises the LLM together with the agentic machinery through which the model acts. The
LLM at the builder’s core lacks these conditions. It is trained across many traditions, so its
learned defaults reflect many coding idioms rather than any one local dialect. The builder
therefore begins with no special commitment to the local tradition. We model these deviations
not as random noise but as a persistent pull toward mainstream patterns learned during
training. We call this pull the bias current. Because in-context instruction does not change the
model’s learned parameters, the local tradition is supplied as context rather than internalized.
Context can shift behavior, but it cannot show that the learned defaults have been replaced, so
the methodology treats the pull as still present. We name this model of the generator the non-
native vernacular builder, a craftsman fluent in many villages’ building styles but native to none,
playing the vernacular builder’s role without the vernacular builder’s enculturation. The harness
governs what this actor produces, and its verdicts do not depend on which internal component
caused a deviation. The prohibition catalog records recurring deviations consistent with the
bias current. Many of the forbidden constructs (@Service annotations, @ActiveProfiles,
Instant.now(), ad-hoc repository methods, javax.* imports) are common Spring Boot or
Java idioms. The catalog marks where mainstream idiom conflicts with local doctrine.

Three design consequences follow:

   1. Enforcement must dominate guidance: the local tradition supplied in the prompt
      competes with the model’s learned defaults. As the context grows or is compacted, that
      guidance cannot be trusted to remain effective. Prose therefore guides generation, but
      gates decide whether the result is acceptable.
   2. Rules guarding anti-mainstream doctrine never retire on statistical grounds: a quiet
      period does not mean that the underlying tendency has disappeared. Observed


                                                24
      compliance does not establish that the model has internalized the local rule, and a new
      model may introduce a different set of recurring violations. Every rule must therefore
      remain demonstrably alive and reachable. It must still fire on a bad fixture, and it must
      still be evaluated on the artifacts a real invocation scans. A rule retires only when a
      change in the local tradition or architecture removes the situation it checks.
   3. The bias current and the capability share a source: the training that creates the pull
      toward the mainstream also supplies the builder’s general competence in Java, Spring
      Boot, and domain-driven design. The local tradition therefore need not restate shared
      knowledge; it encodes the local choices and constraints that the builder’s learned
      defaults do not reliably supply. This lets a tradition of tractable size govern generation.

The generator is globally fluent but locally non-native. The remainder of the methodology is an
engineering response to this asymmetry.

4.2 Primary and proxy context
The builder model says what the generator is. The next question is what it is measured against,
and therefore where the authority for that measurement comes from. Alexander’s context is
“that part of the world which puts demands on this form” [4]. Designers, however, rarely work
from that world directly. They work from mental pictures of it, and those pictures can distort the
structure of the problem. Notes therefore distinguishes the actual world from the designer’s
mental picture and, in turn, from a formal picture in which the problem is expressed as explicit
misfit variables [4]. Our problem-frame specifications, taken together, play a similar
representational role.

The methodology requires one further distinction. Alexander distinguished the world from its
representations. The world ultimately determines whether the system fits, but machines can
check the system only against representations of that world. A representation may be internally
consistent and still fail to represent the world correctly. We therefore distinguish between the
context that ultimately supplies the demands on the system and the representations against
which the system can be mechanically checked: primary versus proxy context.

A primary context is the source of a demand’s authority. The compiler defines what programs it
accepts, the runtime determines how the system actually behaves, and stakeholders decide
whether the system meets their needs. A proxy context represents demands that come from a
primary context. Specifications, pattern documents, templates, and rule catalogs borrow their
authority from the corresponding primary context. Because that authority is borrowed, a proxy
can silently diverge from its source. This can happen in three ordinary ways: the primary
changes and the proxy does not; an update reaches some copies and not others; or the
transcription was wrong from the start. The remedy is reconciliation: recurring checks that
compare a proxy with its primary where the primary can be consulted mechanically, and that
otherwise compare a ruling’s proxies with one another (Table 2).

Not all primary demands have the same origin. Some are settled empirically: the compiler, the
runtime, and the world behave as they behave, regardless of what any document says. Others
are created by authoritative rulings. The primary here is the ruling itself, not the documents that
record it. An architecture decision on the solution side, and a stakeholder ruling on the problem
side, create those demands simply by declaring them. Primary versus proxy is therefore a
distinction of authority, not of medium: a ruling may itself be written down, but the authoritative
act is primary, while copies that derive their authority from it are proxies. A ruling’s primacy
makes it the standard for its proxies, not a fact about the world: whether the demands it creates
still fit the world remains the outer loop’s judgment (S = W). Table 2 labels the two kinds primary
(empirical) and primary (ruling). The distinction applies to both the solution and problem sides

Three practical consequences follow. First, the false-green condition: a form can fit the proxy
while misfitting the primary, so all checks against the proxy can report green while the form
remains wrong. The methodology’s self-audit machinery catches this class of defects where
proxy–primary reconciliation can be mechanized; the outer loop covers the remainder.
Examples include a pattern document that has drifted — a proxy failure — and a stale rule
checker or a gate that passes while checking nothing — a failure of the evaluating machinery,
caught by the fixture and reachability checks of Section 3.4 rather than by reconciliation. A
theory that does not distinguish proxy from primary cannot even express the first kind.

Second, gate density follows the physics of enforcement: gates are concentrated where misfits
do not reveal themselves. The compiler automatically rejects invalid programs. The running
system exposes some defects, but only through tests and the contract clauses they execute. At
the design level, however, the platform itself neither prevents nor exposes violations of
aggregate encapsulation or layering. Those violations therefore require explicit detectors, which
is why the 188-rule catalog is concentrated there.

Third, when a primary cannot be consulted directly, one proxy must serve as the designated
original for mechanical reconciliation. An empirical primary can be consulted directly, so its
proxies are checked against the primary itself: imports against the framework jars and tests
against the live framework. A ruling cannot be consulted this way: its proxies can still be
checked against one another, but their agreement does not establish agreement with the ruling
itself. This is why each rule has one designated original (Section 3.4).

whether the program and its tests match the specification, validation asks whether the
specification matches the world, and assurance requires both; the methodology reserves the
validation judgment to humans (Section 4.3). The equals signs are mnemonic rather than literal:
the model comprises three families of machine-decidable relations enclosed by a fourth
relation that requires human judgment (formal semantics, including the converse check that
nothing undeclared is realized, in Appendix A), a point to which Section 6 returns.

4.3 Detection cost sets the affordable feedback cadence
Distinguishing the two kinds of context says nothing about how often each can be consulted. In
the vernacular, misfit detection was embedded in dwelling rather than performed as a separate
activity, keeping the cost of each additional check low enough for continuous feedback. The
Oregon Experiment instead institutionalized periodic diagnosis [9]. Diagnosis required human
judgment, so it was too costly to perform continuously. The same constraint applies in both
settings: the cost of each additional check sets how often feedback can be afforded (stated as
an inequality in Appendix A).

The methodology makes this constraint explicit in its architecture. The inner loop automates
the checks, so they can be run continuously at low marginal human cost. This allows feedback
at machine speed, restoring the rapid corrective cadence that everyday use provided in the
vernacular. The outer loop, however, ultimately requires human judgment, so its cadence must
be budgeted. A fast review cycle runs at each delivery boundary, where stakeholders observe
the system’s behavior. A slower cycle runs during long-term use, asking whether the system
still serves the people who live with it.

Machines can still help in the outer loop, but their judgments may not substitute for the human
judgment that establishes whether the specification still fits the world. They can remind the
team when no review has happened for too long, gather the evidence for that review, and keep
every verdict recorded and traceable. The methodology reserves that judgment to humans;
machines can check whether it has been made.

Together, these elements recreate the feedback structure of Alexander’s homeostatic process
in engineered form [4]. A vernacular settlement stayed in good fit because people could notice
local misfits and correct them quickly. The dual-loop methodology reproduces this feedback
structure in software. The inner loop repeatedly checks each generated candidate against the
misfit list and drives repair until the detected misfits disappear. The outer loop compares the
specification with the world through human judgment, and it also maintains the
representations and detectors on which the inner loop’s verdicts depend. Domain-driven
decomposition bounds how far repairs propagate, supplying the locality this process requires
(Table 1, P5), but locality alone is not enough. The process works only when manufactured
tradition (Section 3.4), manufactured manifestation (Section 3.5), and mechanized repair
(Section 3.6) work together. Alexander’s homeostatic process therefore corresponds not to any
single pattern but to the verification harness as a whole.

4.4 The severed backflow
The inner loop of Section 4.3 repairs generated artifacts. This section asks who is allowed to
change the tradition itself. In the vernacular, the people who repaired buildings also changed
the tradition, because they belonged to the community that carried it. Repair flowed back into
the tradition. With a builder subject to the bias current, that backflow would be unsafe. A

                                               27
generator allowed to amend the tradition would expose the law itself to the same learned
defaults that the law is meant to constrain.

The theory requires that the governed builder may not, on its own authority, amend the law that
governs it. The methodology therefore assigns the two responsibilities to different actors. The
machine repairs generated artifacts, but it may not amend the tradition. In this methodology,
final legislative authority is reserved to humans, exercised through a deliberate legislative
circuit. Every defect that escapes the gates must then be answered in one of exactly two ways:
by bringing the failure class into the mechanized enumeration 𝑀 of Appendix A — through a new
gate anchored in the law, with a low false-positive rate, or through a repair to an existing
detector, its reachability, or the proxy it reads — or by an explicit ruling that places the failure
class outside 𝑀 and names the means that cover it instead. The first outcome extends or
corrects 𝑀; the second is recorded outside 𝑀, so no claim of machine-checked fit rests on the
class it names.

The hybrid that results embeds the repair dynamic of the unselfconscious process, now
mechanized, in a self-conscious legislature — a configuration, we believe, Alexander did not
describe. It lets each process do what it does best while avoiding what it does poorly. The self-
conscious process is overwhelmed when the interacting misfits grow too complex to treat as a
whole [4], and the unselfconscious tradition adapts only at the pace of cultural change. In the
hybrid, the repair work that would overwhelm a designer is mechanized in the inner loop, and
the tradition can afford to change slowly, because routine artifact repair no longer waits for it.

4.5 The permanent novice
Section 4.1 described the builder at a single generation: biased and native to none. This section
asks not whether the builder can learn the local tradition, but whether it can be shown to have
internalized it well enough for the scaffold to be retired. The Timeless Way ends by saying that
the builder should eventually leave the language behind (Section 2.3). At first, explicit patterns
guide the builder. With experience, the builder comes to respond to the forces directly, and the
scaffold is no longer needed [10]. Alexander’s endpoint presupposes a builder who can
eventually act without continuing dependence on the explicit language.

The methodology must treat the builder as a permanent novice in the local dialect, even though
its underlying model is already an expert in the mainstream. The reason is not that it learns
slowly. It is that observed compliance cannot establish the state Alexander’s forgetting requires:
that the builder can henceforth be trusted to act from the tradition without consulting or being
checked against it.

Alexander’s forgetting is a claim about a state: the builder no longer needs the language and
responds to the forces directly. A generator offers no equivalent warrant for retiring the scaffold.
We can check what it produced this time. We cannot check that it has absorbed the tradition
rather than matched it once. Observing the generator’s output does supply evidence: it can
raise confidence that the learned defaults have shifted. It cannot supply the warrant that
retirement requires: measured reliability is evidence about the builder, not a verdict about any
particular artifact. P1 makes the same point categorically: machine-checked fit rests on
detector verdicts over each artifact, never on generator confidence. Retiring enforcement on
reliability evidence would not establish the artifact claim more cheaply; it would substitute a
claim about the builder for the claim about the artifact. Two further facts push the same way.
The tradition keeps moving through the legislative circuit of Section 3.7, so any internalization
would be a snapshot of one moment. And compliance with stated constraints degrades as the
constraints accumulate, a failure independently documented as constraint decay [22].



                                                28
Alexander’s gate is passed through and eventually left behind (Section 2.3). Here it must be
passed at every generation. Toward the mainstream, the model behaves as if it had already
completed the forgetting that Alexander hoped for. It writes fluent mainstream code without
consulting explicit rules. Its learned defaults supply what the local tradition must supply
explicitly.

A harness is itself a persistent external memory, which raises the obvious objection: if the
builder reads the tradition from files at every generation, has it not remembered it? The answer
separates memory from internalization. Alexander’s forgetting means the builder no longer
needs to consult the language. Our builder must consult it every time, and must then be
checked against it. The memory is real, but it sits outside the builder and is enforced rather than
possessed.

Fine-tuning is the strongest form of the objection, because it moves the dialect into the weights
rather than keeping it in a file. It may reduce how much of the tradition must be supplied as
guidance (Section 8), but it gives no grounds for retiring verification. A retrained model carries
learned defaults as of one training run; evaluation can show that those defaults have shifted,
not that any particular subsequent generation will comply with the tradition. A retrained builder
is still a generator whose learned reliability does not establish the compliance of any particular
output, so its output requires verification against the governing tradition (Section 4.1).

Humans, by contrast, can follow Alexander’s original path. With repeated use, the specification
language can become part of their ordinary practice instead of something they consciously
consult. What internalization retires, however, is guidance, not verification. The machinery of
Sections 3.4–3.7 is therefore not temporary scaffolding. It is a permanent institution: even
evidence that the generator had completed the same learning process would not discharge the
obligation to verify each artifact (P1).

Some obligations do not arise specifically from the builder being an LLM and therefore match
long-standing practice (P1, P2, P4, P5, P7). Others take on their present force because no one
can establish that an LLM builder has internalized the tradition, because it defaults to the
mainstream, and because it can be guided only through representations (P3, P6, P9, P10). P8
sits between the two groups: any builder faces a residue of judgment, but mechanizing the
builder forces that residue outside the autonomous inner loop. For the second group and for P8,
that force follows from what the builder is, not from arbitrary methodological choices.


5. Empirical Evidence
The theory is instantiated in a single system. We do not claim that the architectural binding is
universal. We claim only that one working system implements a mechanism for every obligation
the theory derives. Section 8 explains what this single case does and does not show.

The reference implementation implements the core domain of a Scrum-management system
(products, sprints, product backlog items, and Scrum teams). It is built with domain-driven
design, Clean Architecture, event sourcing, CQRS, and design by contract on Spring Boot/Java,
together with a React/TypeScript frontend. Generation and verification ran inside an agentic
coding environment (Claude Code, Anthropic), with the harness implemented as a plugin that
attaches the gates and hooks to the environment’s extension points.

Executable contracts are enforced at runtime by the uContract library [23]. The library, like the
rest of the supporting framework family, belongs to the local dialect rather than to the
mainstream. The builder cannot be assumed to know these APIs, so the written tradition must
supply them (Section 4.1).

                                                29
Development began in July 2025 and remains ongoing. During this period, generation used
Claude Sonnet, Opus, and Fable models, and each change of builder model was followed by a
fresh rebuild. The system is not a re-implementation of existing software. Its specifications
were developed from scratch through Event Storming and problem-frame analysis (Section 3.2).
Its backend has been built, and repeatedly rebuilt, from empty source directories using only the
methodology. All 64 problem-frame specifications across four aggregates were executed under
the full gate stack.

The rebuilds also exercised the harness itself. Whenever the gates or patterns changed, we
deleted all previously generated source code and regenerated the backend from scratch. Every
rebuilt backend then had to pass the end-to-end test suite, which is maintained by hand and
never regenerated with the backend, driven through the unchanged frontend. To the extent
captured by its assertions, the suite served as a regression oracle held fixed across rebuilds, in
the manner of a characterization test [24]: behavior accepted in one rebuild had to remain
compatible in the next, so each new rebuild was checked not only against the specifications
but also against the accepted behavior of earlier rebuilds. After a series of rebuilds was
complete, we manually ran an endpoint-coverage check to confirm that the suite exercised
every endpoint declared in the specifications. The backend grows one use case at a time, while
these checks exercise the whole system. We therefore run them only after a complete rebuild,
outside the fix loop.

The frontend itself was vibe-coded once, accepted through direct human validation, and was
not regenerated thereafter. This division of assurance labor follows the economics of Section
4.3: an artifact generated once can afford human-priced validation, while the endlessly
regenerated backend cannot. Table 3 summarizes the specification corpus, the written
tradition and verification harness, the generated backend, and the rebuild outcomes. Every

The observations fall into three families. Together, they exercise several of the theory’s
propositions rather than evaluate it exhaustively.

Observation 1 (P1 negative fit, P2 manifestation; P6 tradition-bearer, P8 residue of
judgment). Locally well-formed artifacts can still misfit at their boundaries; boundary
escapes exposed gaps in the enumeration. The closeout of each rebuild performs a final
verification pass over the whole aggregate. It repeatedly found defects that no per-artifact
inspection had caught. Examples included an input field that the receiving service silently never
reads, a controller error branch that can never execute and therefore collapses specified errors
into HTTP 500s, a mapper that loses derived state when reconstructing an aggregate from
storage, and a specified cross-aggregate reaction that was never wired. Each artifact is locally
well-formed, so these defects remain invisible when inspected one artifact at a time. The misfit
lies in the chain between them (Section 3.5).

Under the two-way answer of Section 4.4, every escape has exactly two permissible outcomes.
It either extends or corrects the enumeration or receives an explicit ruling that places it outside
𝑀 and names the non-gate mechanism that covers it.

Examples of new gates include checks for input-field consumption chains, controller error-
branch reachability, boundary-value contract tests, and value-object nullability. Semantic
faithfulness to the specification’s intent, beyond what the deterministic gates already capture,
cannot be decided by the harness. The methodology therefore assigns it to the advisory multi-
model review of Section 3.5, not to the deterministic fit predicate; the review’s findings go to
human adjudication (Section 3.6).

Quietly fixing the individual defect and moving on is not permitted. When the misfit list proves
incomplete, human judgment re-enters through post-mortem analysis to extend the
enumeration, as the residue-of-judgment concession of Section 2.1 predicts.

Observation 2 (P2 manifestation, P10 formal picture). Proxies drift, and so do the detectors
themselves. Independent audits of the verification artifacts found the same failure class at
multiple levels. Documentation reported counts that no longer matched the rules. Hooks still
checked retired doctrine. Gates passed only because their scan set had silently narrowed. A

Two of these failures, the stale counts and the stale docstring, are examples of the proxy drift

scan sets, are failures of the evaluating machinery itself (Section 4.2). Each failure led to a new

regularly against fixtures that show the rules still fire and against real invocations that show the
rules are still reached.

Observation 3 (P1 negative fit, P3 viscosity). Prompt-carried guidance was repeatedly
violated; the corresponding gates caught the violations. The project’s own history showed
this observation early on. The project began with prose alone: a large collection of prompts and
sample code with no executable checks. With prose alone, the same classes of rule violations
continued to recur.

We therefore added deterministic gates one by one. The model wrote each gate, but the
authors decided what it should check. The written tradition evolved in the same way. The
authors wrote the first core patterns by hand. After the system had stabilized, the model drafted
later pattern documents from sample code supplied by the authors, who again decided what
should be checked. This arrangement does not delegate normative authority to the model: the
decision of what to check remains with the authors (Principle 6), and the detectors themselves
are verified so that the implementation of that decision stays faithful (Observation 2).

We then substituted other models of the same family, while keeping the harness unchanged;
these were working rebuilds rather than controlled comparisons (Section 8). Prompt-carried
rules were violated repeatedly, but the corresponding gates caught those violations. The same
pattern appeared in maintaining the proxies. Session-start reconciliation checks repeatedly
detected proxy drift before generation began, for example by comparing pattern imports with
framework jars, versions with manifests, and network ports with the designated configuration
file. These observations are consistent with the theoretical claim that enforcement, not
guidance, must carry compliance (Section 4.1).

Taken together, these observations show that the methodology can repeatedly regenerate
implementations of the same specified system that remain compatible with the behavior
exercised by the fixed end-to-end suite, while its verification machinery continues to evolve.
The observations therefore exercise the theory in operation rather than on a benchmark.

6. Answering Alexander’s Challenge
Alexander’s OOPSLA address [5] posed a graded challenge: is your language generative; does it
aim at wholeness; does it make life better?

Generativity: answered operationally by supplying the missing executor. In the three
decades since patterns entered software, mainstream practice has used them mainly as
reference literature: read and applied through professional judgment rather than executed as a
written generative procedure. Deterministic generators, from templates to model-driven and
grammar-based synthesis, could execute a pattern only after its operative content had been
rendered as formal rules, with the prose and its rationale left as documentation the generator
never read. The LLM and the harness together supply both at once: the model interprets a
pattern language as written, and the harness governs its output deterministically. The
governance is necessary precisely because interpretation does not guarantee compliance
(Observation 3). Under it, the language demonstrably generates. A specification goes in, a
structurally uniform system verified against the encoded obligations comes out, and the
process is repeatable enough to rebuild a system from empty directories. Those thirty years
were not wasted, however. They left a written tradition cataloged across scales, from problem
classification [12] through architecture and design [14, 16, 17, 21] to acceptance scenarios [26].



                                                       34
A team manufacturing its tradition today curates a mature literature instead of first writing one.
Alexander, in building, had to articulate his own from a tradition that was still largely tacit.

This execution model also reproduces an emphasis in Alexander’s later work on generative
sequences of structure-preserving steps [27, Book 2]. Each problem-frame type’s workflow is
an engineering analogue of such a sequence: a path through the pattern network of Section 3.3,
unfolded layer by layer, with regression guards verifying that each transformation preserved the
machine-checked properties previous steps established. The analogue is bounded: the guards
preserve machine-checked properties, not Alexander’s living structure.

Coherence: answered in the formal sense, open in the experiential sense. Aggregate-wide,
cross-frame, and cross-aggregate gates check that the parts compose into a consistent whole,
coherence in the formal sense. Alexander’s felt wholeness [27] is a different property —
structural in his account, not a matter of opinion — and we make no claim to have mechanized
it.

We observe instead that when routine construction and repair are delegated to machines, as
they are here, code is no longer the artifact that people routinely read, modify, and reason
about. The level at which people work moves up, a shift consistent with recent spec-driven
work [28]. On our account, people now inhabit the activities that frame and govern construction:
discovering the problem, formalizing it into specifications, and maintaining the tradition that
governs what the machine builds. Moving up does not make the code alien, however. The four
forms confine the builder to the team’s encoded tradition (Section 3.4), so what the machine
writes remains within the team’s architectural vernacular. When someone does step down to
the code, to adjudicate an escape for example, they are reading code constrained to conform to
their own encoded tradition. The claim is not that generated code is intrinsically easier to
understand, which would require empirical study. The claim is that enforced tradition preserves
a shared architectural vernacular across machine-generated artifacts, keeping the system
culturally recognizable to the maintainers who will eventually inspect or modify it. In this sense,
governance serves verification and preserves a precondition for habitability [29]. One form of
the quality-without-a-name question (in software terms: is this a structure a person can inhabit,
revise, and think in?) therefore arises most immediately at the frames rather than at the
generated code. That question is open, and no gate should pretend to close it.

The moral dimension: located but not mechanized. Every machine-verified relation in the
methodology relates artifacts to artifacts: code to specification, specification to schema,
detector to fixture. The relation Alexander actually cares about (does the structure nourish the
lives lived with it?) holds between the built thing and the world. Because the machine-checked
legs already tie the program to its specification, the model carries what remains of that relation
as S = W, specification against world. Section 4.2 shows why no machine verdict reaches it. The
problem-side primary cannot be summoned into a test harness. The outer loop of Figure 2
reserves the slot for this judgment. The limit of this answer is that a slot is not yet an institution.

Alexander did not merely argue that human judgment matters. He also proposed institutional
forms for exercising it. One is the Oregon Experiment’s diagnosis [9]: periodic, formatted,
pattern-anchored, budget-connected human judgment of the built world’s health. Translated to
software, the theory calls for an institution of this kind; we propose a scheduled inhabitation
review as one such mechanism. Its findings would be recorded, traced to the frames they
implicate, and fed back into amendments of the governing representations. Machines supply
the reminders, the evidence, and the records (Section 4.3). Humans supply the verdicts. One
participatory practice already exists at discovery time, the Event Storming workshop of Section
3.2; but at present, the participation of non-engineers is not carried forward into formalization.



                                                  35
The economics of Section 4.3 make the outer loop slower and more expensive than the inner
one. The ethics of Alexander’s OOPSLA address say the priority runs the other way: the inner
loop exists to serve the outer. Making the three machine-decidable families of S = P = T = W
routine (Appendix A) is not the goal in itself. Its purpose is to free human attention for the fourth
relation, S = W, the one that requires human judgment. The engineer’s work does not vanish in
this division. It relocates, to the outer loop’s contact with the world the software serves, and to
the building of the tradition and harness themselves. Taken together, the three answers are
consistent: mechanize what can be checked, relocate what cannot be checked, and do not let
the machinery substitute for the judgment.


7. Related Work
Alexander’s software reception. The software-patterns movement took shape through Beck
and Cunningham’s early adaptation of Alexander [30], the Gang of Four catalog [16], and the
Pattern Languages of Program Design community [31]. Gabriel [29] kept questions of
habitability, piecemeal growth, and Alexandrian generativity in view (questions that much of the
catalog-oriented reception treated only weakly), and Section 6 follows that line of inquiry. In our
view, this tradition generally lacked not insight but a governable executor. Patterns could teach
their users, but their application still depended on human interpretation and judgment. Even
when a language prescribed the order in which its patterns apply, that grammar was interpreted
by its users rather than enforced and executed as a machine-verifiable generative process. The
four-form obligation (Section 3.4) is what becomes of a pattern when its reader can no longer
be trusted to exercise situated judgment consistently.

Specification and contract traditions. Jackson’s problem frames [12] supply the classificatory
basis of our problem representation; Meyer’s design by contract [13] supplies its executable
contract layer; and behavior-driven development [26] supplies the Given–When–Then scenario
grammar that our acceptance specifications formalize and machine-check. Recent evidence
suggests that making preconditions and postconditions explicit can improve the accuracy of
LLM code generation [32]. Evans’s domain-driven design [14] supplies the principal solution-
side vocabulary, supplemented by Vernon’s treatment of aggregates, domain events, and
event-sourced implementation [19], and arranged within the layered macro-structure and
dependency discipline of Martin’s Clean Architecture [21]. Mutation testing [25, 33] serves, in
our stack, as a meta-level adequacy check over executable verification, checking whether the
tests, and the contract clauses they execute, detect deliberately injected defects. Taken
together, these traditions contribute problem classification, executable contracts, acceptance
scenarios, architectural vocabulary and structure, and tests of verification adequacy. None of
them, as conventionally combined, provides a criterion for when an AI-coding verification
harness is sufficient to justify a stated claim of fit. This is the question addressed by the paper’s
misfit calculus (P1; Appendix A).

Recent specification-driven work on AI-assisted development [34] goes further by formalizing
specification governance as a reference model. In this model, structured specifications serve
as the authoritative source of truth, stochastic generation must pass deterministic validation,
and generation, verification, and governance close into a loop. This specification-centered
approach has an industrial counterpart in Spec Kit [35], which, in the version examined here,
externalizes intent into persistent, structured artifacts rather than leaving it only in
conversational prompts: a constitution declares project principles, an ordered path runs from
specification through plan and tasks to implementation, cross-artifact analysis checks those
representations for consistency, and a convergence step checks the resulting code against
them. Both lines structure the path from specification to implementation. Our theory addresses


                                                 36
a logically prior question of sufficiency: what must the verification boundary around such a path
establish before the absence of detected misfits can justify a claim of fit? The question remains
because the specification and the detectors that enforce it may themselves misrepresent or
drift from their sources. A claim of fit is justified only relative to the misfits that have been
explicitly identified and checked. The primary–proxy distinction makes the harness’s own
failure modes expressible, and the legislative circuit governs their repair. Read against Table 1,
a workflow of this kind could be bound into an implementation of the theory; the table states
the obligations it would still have to discharge, among them which constitutional constraints
must be operationalized as detectors, how the manifestation of each declared misfit is
manufactured and its detector kept live, and how amendments to the law are kept outside the
autonomous fix loop.

The sufficiency question has a close neighbor in safety-critical systems. Assurance cases
justify critical claims through structured arguments over evidence [36], and eliminative
approaches build confidence by identifying and resolving defeaters, the ways in which those
claims or their supporting arguments could fail [37]. This negative logic is closely related to
misfit enumeration. Like misfit enumeration, eliminative induction makes confidence
conditional on the identified ways in which a claim could fail rather than on an absolute proof
that none remain. The difference lies in what that logic must become in a generative workflow.
In Assurance 2.0 and eliminative approaches, defeaters remain part of an assurance argument
to be assessed. The misfit calculus requires each misfit in the mechanized enumeration to be
embodied in a deterministic detector whose liveness is itself verified, so that adjudication can
occur at the rate at which the builder produces candidates (Section 4.3).

LLM code generation, repair, and guardrails. Code-generation benchmarks such as
HumanEval [38] evaluate function-level code synthesis, while repository-level benchmarks
such as SWE-bench [39] evaluate whether systems can resolve real-world GitHub issues in
existing codebases. SWE-agent [40] shifts attention from the model alone to the design of the
agent’s working environment, showing that an agent-computer interface can substantially
affect coding performance. These works study coding-task completion under an externally
supplied evaluation oracle, whether generating a function or repairing a repository. Our theory
instead addresses generating systems from explicit specifications, where the oracle is part of
the problem rather than given in advance (Contribution 1). The evaluation target differs as well:
these benchmarks ask whether the code completes the task, while fit asks whether the
candidate exhibits any enumerated misfit — functional failure is only one class among them
(Section 3.5).

Self-repair and self-debugging systems [41, 42] mechanize iterative correction using failing
tests, execution feedback, or the model’s own explanations of its generated code. They study
how such feedback enables repair, including how self-generated explanation can support
debugging even without external feedback on code correctness [42] and how feedback quality
limits self-repair [41]. They do not ask how a verification harness should be constituted, kept
live and reconciled with its sources, and amended when an escape reveals that the
enumeration is incomplete (P1, P2, P6, P10). Once a gate has detected a manifested misfit,
these mechanisms are compatible with our theory as repair strategies inside the fix loop
(Section 3.6). They can make repair more effective, but they do not replace the gate stack that
determines which misfits are made manifest and detected in the first place.

Reflective agents [43] let a model learn from feedback on its own attempts within a task, storing
verbal reflections to improve subsequent trials. Their feedback loop serves task performance.
Our theory instead institutionalizes the outer loop, in which humans judge whether the
specification itself still fits the world (S = W; P7, P8). A related line of work moves behavioral
constraints outside the model altogether. NeMo Guardrails [44] expresses programmable rails

                                               37
around LLM applications. AgentSpec [45] provides a domain-specific language for specifying
and enforcing runtime constraints on agent behavior. ContextCov [46] transforms
operationalizable constraints in agent instruction files into executable checks. These systems
reinforce the premise that natural-language instructions alone do not reliably ensure
compliance. Where constraints can be operationalized, executable enforcement makes
compliance more directly checkable than instruction alone. Our question is one of sufficiency.
We ask what makes a set of constraints sufficient to justify a claim of fit, how the proxies and
detectors that encode those constraints are kept live and reconciled with their sources, and
who may amend them when an escape reveals that the enumeration is incomplete. Across
these approaches, the same residual risk remains. A generated artifact can pass every existing
check while still violating requirements that no check covers. Our theory does not eliminate this
risk. Fit is never claimed absolutely but only against a stated enumeration (P1, P8; Appendix A),
and every escape must be answered, by extending or correcting the enumeration or by an
explicit ruling (Section 4.4).

Repository-scale coherence and specification-driven verification. Four recent works
approach parts of this territory from complementary directions. Structured spec-driven
engineering [28] argues that structured specifications can guide repository-level generation
while making its results easier to verify. Constraint decay [22] shows that coding agents
become less reliable as structural requirements accumulate while the API contract is held fixed.
This independently documents a failure pattern that the non-native builder model predicts
(Sections 4.1 and 4.5). The patchwork problem [47] names a failure class our closeout gates
also target: code that is locally valid but globally incoherent. It detects this failure
deterministically by checking consistency invariants over graphs derived from the repository’s
own artifacts. It detects rather than repairs. CodeSpec [48] has an LLM build repository-
grounded functional chains from feature requirements and repository evidence, compile them
into executable architecture and behavior specifications, and check the implementation
against them. Taken individually, these mechanisms are therefore no longer unique to any
single system. Three differences remain. Our chains originate in pre-existing specifications
rather than in the repository’s own structure, so the checks compare the code with declared
meaning, not only with itself [47]. Our specification language is law that the builder may not
amend, whereas a specification the generator derives for itself [48] remains open to the
generator’s own bias current (Section 4.4). And our field gates trace each field the specification
declares, checking name, type, and genuine carriage at every representation it crosses (Section
3.5).

Beyond particular interfaces and enforcement mechanisms, recent work increasingly treats the
harness itself as a first-class engineering object [49]. One line catalogs the runtime
responsibilities a harness must cover around a foundation-model agent [2]. Another evolves
harnesses automatically against observed failures [3]. A third asks what makes a system a
harness at all, giving the category an operational membership test [50]. A parallel strand
engineers the process that drives the agent, not only the software it produces. One line of work
formalizes that process as an explicit, written loop specification handed to the agent harness
[51]. Another demands proof before lifecycle progress: each transition requires fresh, state-
bound, machine-checkable evidence, and unsupported claims are not admitted [52]. Our
contribution is complementary but distinct. Existing work has shown that agent performance
depends on interface design, that natural-language constraints can be externalized into
executable enforcement, that harness responsibilities can be cataloged and harnesses can be
automatically evolved, and that the agent’s loop can itself be specified and evidence-gated. We
derive instead the obligations of a verification harness from Alexander’s theory of form. The
resulting sufficiency claim is relative to the mechanized misfit enumeration of Appendix A. The
question is not merely which runtime components are useful, but what must be established


                                               38
before passing every existing check can justify a claim of fit. Enforcement alone is not enough:
the detectors must remain live, the proxies they check must remain reconciled with their
sources, the repair of detected misfits must be mechanized, and the enumeration itself must
be governed. Automatic harness evolution [3] also sharpens a governance question our theory
makes explicit: when the harness rewrites itself, is it improving a mechanism or amending the
norm that defines correctness? P6 requires that the second kind of change pass through the
human-gated legislative circuit.

External validity. Section 5 noted that all evidence comes from one project, one architecture,
one solution stack, and one primary legislator of the tradition. The theory’s portability claim is
precise and falsifiable, and it prescribes its own test. A second MGD binding holds the problem
fixed (its stakeholder demands, classified by the same frame types; as Section 3.2 notes, the
problem representation is architecture-dependent and may change), rebinds the solution side
to a different architecture, technology stack, or implementation language, and asks whether
the same obligations can again be discharged by different mechanisms. The present problem-
frame artifacts separate the problem side from the solution side only in part. Gates already
keep each frame’s frame.yaml from naming any solution file outside the argument it records
for each frame concern, and require the frame’s requirements to be written in the vocabulary of
the world, while a separate binding file, outside the five kinds of artifact listed in Section 3.2,
names the architecture-dependent specifications (its machine and domain specifications) that
realize the frame; those arguments and the acceptance scenarios, however, still cite solution-
side artifacts and use the solution family’s vocabulary. Completing the separation would
confine solution-dependent changes to those specifications, making “holding the problem
fixed” a property the harness can check rather than a methodological intention. Such a binding
falsifies the theory if an obligation can be shown to be undischargeable in a binding that
otherwise meets the theory’s premises, or if the conditions from which the theory derives an
obligation hold and the obligation can be removed without loss of the property it was derived to
protect. This is not the only route: the derivation can also fail on logical grounds, if an obligation
does not follow from its premises (Section 2.5). Because the derivation fixes the obligations but
not the mechanisms, the failure of a particular implementation does not by itself count against
the obligation it was meant to discharge.

A second binding may vary the implementation technology, the architecture, or both; varying
the architecture reaches deeper. The evaluation fixes one solution-side family (domain-driven
design, Clean Architecture, CQRS, and event sourcing), but Table 1 lists such choices only
among the mechanisms, never among the obligations. A different family, say a CRUD-oriented
three-tier architecture, would keep the frame types, which classify the problem rather than the
solution, but would need its own specification schemas, decomposition units, pattern network,
and gate set, while on the theory’s account the obligations stand. Like the portability claim, this
prediction is untested. We evaluated this family because it is the tradition we have built on
since 2018 (Section 3.3) and because it is a constraint-rich case: its aggregate boundaries,
inward-only dependencies, separate command and query models, and event-sourced state
give the pattern network more to encode and the gates more to enforce. The theory prescribes
how fit to a chosen context must be verified, not what that context must contain; a project that
excludes architectural structure from its context thereby removes rules from its law, not
obligations from the theory.

The obligations of Table 1 were derived from the classical theory plus one premise about the
builder, so their claim to generality stands or falls with the derivation and its premises, not with
the case. The case shows that the obligations can be jointly discharged in at least one working
system: feasibility, not portability. Replication is therefore needed to test whether the
obligations can be discharged elsewhere and how far the premises hold empirically, not
whether the derivation is logically valid. The second-binding experiment is stated precisely so
that any group can run it without us; we also intend to run it ourselves on a second architecture
and technology stack.

Builder variation. The rebuilds were not designed to isolate the effect of model capability from
that of the harness; they exercised the same obligations under successive builders. So long as

                                                 40
the builder premise holds, changing the builder may change the violation profile, but it does not
thereby change the obligations, which attach to the builder premise rather than to any
particular model. Observation 3 shows that the violations were not peculiar to one model or
model version, though all observed builders came from one model family; rebuilds with hosted
and locally run LLMs from other families are the natural next test of that claim. How violation
profiles differ across builders is a further question. The deterministic-review telemetry records
every violation by rule, with severity, file, and timestamp, and Section 5 reports a first analysis.
Counted per rule and per model version and normalized by each rule’s evaluation opportunities,
this record would map how the bias current manifests under a fixed law and harness, and
would make a model swap under a fixed problem set and generation protocol auditable: a
measured shift rather than an unverified assumption. Fine-tuning a model on the pattern
documents is one more builder change to measure this way, and a possible mechanical
analogue of Alexander’s re-internalization (Section 2.3; Table 1, P9). We conjecture that it
would thin the prompted guidance; Section 4.5 explains why it gives no grounds for retiring the
gates.

Of the three machine-decidable relations, S = T is the least directly verified. The LLM
currently translates each acceptance scenario into a test, and machines check the result only
structurally: every scenario not deferred by the specification must have a corresponding test
whose assertions match the kind of outcome its then-clause declares, and a state or quoted
value the clause names must appear in that scenario’s test method. No machine checks
whether the test verifies what the scenario actually meant. The program is likewise generated
by the LLM from the specification, but it is checked against the specification twice, directly by
the structural gates of S = P and indirectly by running the tests; the tests are checked only
structurally, yet they are the oracle by which the program’s behavior is judged (Appendix A.2). A
misreading of the specification that the model carries into both the program and its tests can
therefore pass unnoticed, unless it also breaks a structural agreement that the S = P gates
check. Mutation scores do not help here: they measure whether the tests detect changes in the
program, a relation between tests and program only, and a test suite can reach a high, even
complete, mutation score under the chosen operators while asserting the wrong business rule.
The one mechanism aimed directly at semantic faithfulness, the multi-model review of Section
3.5, remained advisory. This acknowledged residual risk is addressed directly only by a
mechanism whose effect has not been measured. Placing that review outside the misfit set 𝑀
(Appendix A.1) keeps this honest, since no claim of fit rests on it, but the gap in coverage is real.

We see three remedies, ordered from cheap to structural. First, trace every expected value a
then-clause declares to an assertion of the corresponding test. A weaker form already blocks: a
named state or quoted value must appear somewhere in the scenario’s test method, while
numbers and dates are compared only as warnings. Requiring each value to reach an assertion
extends the field gates of Section 3.5 one hop further, from the data transfer object to the
assertion; it cannot show that the assertion constrains the right subject or expresses the
intended relation, but it catches an expected value that reaches no assertion at all. Second,
mutate the specification, the mirror image of program mutation: perturb one expected value,
regenerate only the test, and run it against the unchanged program; the test should now fail.
While tests are generated by an LLM, a surviving mutant may reveal a clause that no assertion
constrains or mere variation in the regenerated test, and a verdict this irreproducible can only
advise (Section 3.5). Third, make the translation deterministic. A translator that derives each
test, assertions included, from the acceptance specification turns the S = T leg from “generated
then approximately checked” into “mechanically derived.” Trust then concentrates in the
translator, which must be verified like the rest of the harness (P10; Section 4.2), and the
specification must express expected values formally enough to compile. The difficulty thus
moves into a form the methodology already addresses through misfit enumeration and the


                                                 41
specification linter. Once the third is in place and the mutation operators are restricted to
mechanically valid perturbations whose expected outcome contradicts the original, so that no
program can satisfy both the scenario and its mutant, the second can be promoted from advice
to a gate.

Appendix A demands more than the implementation uniformly delivers. The gate runner
enforces 𝑀!"#$ = 𝑀 at gate granularity: a gate that cannot run is counted as skipped, never as
passed. Within a gate, however, a detector whose scan set silently narrows can still pass
without evaluating everything it should, and vacuously if the set is empty. The audits of
Observation 2 caught this class, and several gates now fail loudly on an empty scan set, but
uniform reporting of |𝑀\𝑀!"#$ | at predicate granularity remains future engineering.
The blocking gates enclose only the rebuilt backend. The React frontend and the backend
each hold their own picture of the HTTP contract, and no blocking detector compared their
fields. Field names, date formats, and enum vocabularies drifted apart while unit tests and
static types on both sides stayed green: two proxies of one truth, each checked only against
itself, the class of failure that Section 4.2 predicts. Only end-to-end execution made the drift
visible. A deep check that compares the specification, the backend controllers, and the
frontend calls field by field exists but runs only as an opt-in audit; a shallower check, that the
endpoints the frontend calls exist at all, runs as an edit-time hook. Extending the enumeration
across this boundary is one direction in which the misfit list is currently growing.

The economics of externalization. We measured neither the effort of building the harness nor
that of authoring the specifications. The theory expects the harness effort to be substantial: the
harness carries the portion of the tradition that the builder cannot be assumed to know, and
Section 4.5 argues that this scaffold cannot come down. Two economies bound the harness
cost. The tradition needs to encode chiefly what the builder’s learned defaults do not reliably
supply (Section 4.1), although what to verify remains governed by the enumeration, not by the
builder’s reliability; and the frame concerns are amortized across every problem of a class
(Section 2.4). Whether the cost pays for itself, within one project or across projects on the same
stack, is unmeasured, and the deterministic-review telemetry above measures only
interception, one input to the benefit side. Specification authoring is a cost paid per use case; it
replaces prompt-writing and routine code review, and its judgment-bearing material is roughly
the quarter of the corpus that Section 5 locates. The theory predicts not that this cost is small,
only that it buys what prompt effort alone cannot: an externalized specification can anchor
independent machine verification. A purpose-built specification editor could keep the class
diagram drawn during Event Storming and the specification files synchronized in both
directions, so that a developer would never need to edit the YAML directly. Because the price
amortizes over repeated regeneration, it buys little in exploratory prototypes, in one-off tools, or
while the architecture itself is still being discovered; the reference implementation draws this
boundary itself, governing and regenerating its backend while its frontend was vibe-coded once
(Section 5).

A third cost is the context window. We cannot yet say how much content the guidance forms
(doctrine, template, and prohibitions), and the doctrine in particular, should carry: content
beyond what correctness needs competes for the context that generation itself uses. In one
informal comparison, a student group working by mob programming hand-wrote the guidance
forms for one layer; generation worked under them, and they came to about half the length of
their model-drafted counterparts but took far longer to write. Our working explanation is that a
human author recognizes better which local choices the builder’s learned defaults do not
supply (Section 4.1). In this project the boundary between needed and excess content was
found by trial, the most time-consuming part of forming the pattern language, and no principle


                                                42
yet predicts it. The gates face no such limit, because they run outside the generation context:
one more asymmetry between guidance and enforcement.

The outer loop as institution. Section 6 called for institutions for the human outer loop; none
has been built yet. Three pieces of design work remain: scheduled inhabitation reviews, in
which humans periodically judge whether the system still serves the people who live with it; a
field in each frame specification recording whose work the frame is supposed to improve, so far
drafted for only a few frames, so that an inhabitation review can later ask whether it actually did;
and the participation of non-engineers in authoring frames. This is where the methodology must
grow if it is to answer the moral dimension of Alexander’s challenge, not only its generativity
and coherence.


9. Conclusion
Alexander’s 1964 account [4] implies that, to the extent that the relevant misfits can be
enumerated and mechanically detected, synthesis reduces to their technical elimination. That
implication has become operational with the arrival of a builder productive enough to be worth
governing, yet not to be trusted without verification. This paper has derived the harness’s
obligations from the classical theory, proposition by proposition, under the changed builder
premise (Table 1). Under that premise, the broken enabling conditions of the vernacular
become explicit engineering obligations. In this binding, misfit enumeration becomes a gate
stack, tradition becomes a four-form pattern language, and vernacular repair becomes a
regression-guarded fix loop. The methodology that results is Misfit-Governed Development: the
builder proposes candidate forms, and the enumerated misfits govern what may be accepted.
In this view, harness engineering is not a collection of ad hoc techniques but a discipline whose
obligations follow from a single account of fit. One working system shows that these obligations
can be jointly discharged; whether they can be discharged in other bindings is the test of
Section 8. The residue of judgment (P8) stays where the derived theory places it: in the human
outer loop, which judges whether the specification fits the world and approves every extension
and amendment of the misfit enumeration. The machine’s inner loop can be made routine and
uneventful, and this is the point. Making it routine frees human attention for the outer loop,
where Alexander’s question remains: whether the software ultimately serves human life.

Appendix A. A Minimal Formal Vocabulary
We fix a minimal formal vocabulary for what gains precision by it: the misfit calculus, the
semantics of the S = P = T = W model, and the detection-cost bound. The vernacular and
homeostasis material of Sections 2 and 4 is deliberately left in prose; formalizing it would add
notation without adding precision.

A.1 Misfits and fit
Adapting the formal apparatus of Notes [4] to this setting, let 𝐺 be the governing representation
that a fix-loop window holds fixed: the specifications and the tradition against which candidates
are mechanically checked (P4). 𝐺 is a proxy context in the sense of Section 4.2; the world 𝑊
stays outside it. Let 𝐴 be the set of candidate artifact states 𝑎, each comprising a generated
form and its generated tests. The governing representation and the permanent verification
artifacts are not part of 𝑎: their validity is a meta-level admissibility condition, stated below.
Notes summarizes the state of each potential misfit by a binary variable; for a fixed 𝐺, we carry
each mechanized misfit into the calculus as a misfit predicate: a decidable predicate 𝑚%& : 𝐴 →
{0,1}, where 𝑚%& (𝑎) = 1 means that artifact state 𝑎 exhibits misfit 𝑖 against 𝐺. The enumeration
is the finite set 𝑀 = {𝑚'& , … , 𝑚(& }: each 𝑚%& formalizes one mechanized misfit class of the law
and is evaluated by a blocking checker in the harness. Fit is the derived, negative notion:

                                fit ),& (𝑎) ⇔ ∀𝑚 ∈ 𝑀: 𝑚(𝑎) = 0.

Making 𝐺 a parameter rather than part of the artifact state records P4 formally: within a fix-loop
window, successive candidates are judged against the same target, which is what makes one-
at-a-time repair possible (Section 3.6); any amendment to 𝐺 ends the window and re-enters
through the legislature of Section 3.7.

Two properties follow immediately from the definition; the third is an operational qualification.
First, fit is relative to the enumeration: fit ),& (𝑎) asserts nothing about predicates outside 𝑀;
this is the formal boundedness relevant to P8, and the reason the enumeration remains open.
Second, fit is decidable because every 𝑚%& is: the autonomous inner loop is possible because
each 𝑚%& was engineered to be a decidable predicate (P2); in the vernacular, physical
consequences instead made many misfits directly manifest. Third, fit is relative to the subset
actually evaluated: let 𝑀!"#$ ⊆ 𝑀 be the misfit predicates a given run successfully evaluates.
Each 𝑚%& is total; the detector that evaluates it is not. A detector unable to resolve its subject —
a scan set that matched no file or silently missed some, a specification in a format it does not
parse — leaves its predicate unevaluated rather than evaluated to 0. What a run establishes is
fit )!"#$ ,& (𝑎); the claim fit ),& (𝑎) is justified only when 𝑀!"#$ = 𝑀, so a harness that reports fit
without also reporting |𝑀\𝑀!"#$ | claims more than it checked. The first property says the
enumeration may be incomplete; this one says it may be incompletely applied, and the
difference matters because a report that counts only the verdicts returned cannot show it.
Recognition and mechanization are also distinct: the humans who govern the harness can
recognize a misfit class, and rule on it under the two-way answer of Section 4.4, while the class
still admits no decidable predicate; 𝑀 contains only the mechanized classes. The optional
multi-model review of Section 3.5 deliberately sits outside 𝑀: it probes aspects of semantic
faithfulness for which the methodology has no deterministic decision predicate. The advisory
gates of Section 3.5 likewise sit outside 𝑀: their warnings inform the human legislature but do
not enter the fit predicate.

Two boundary notes place the calculus. First, 𝐺 and 𝑀 together correspond to what Section 3
calls the law: 𝐺 is its representational part — including the records of rulings on classes not
mechanized — and 𝑀 formalizes its mechanized misfit enumeration. Second, fit ),& is an
object-level predicate on candidates. P10 requires two kinds of checks on the representations:
checks on 𝐺 itself (the machine-checked specification syntax and the partially machine-
checked semantics of Section 3.2) and checks that reconcile proxies with their primaries
(Section 4.2). These checks, together with the determinism of every checker (Section 3.5) and
the fixture and reachability checks that show each detector still fires and is still reached (P2,
P10; Sections 3.4 and 5), are meta-level admissibility conditions on the representations and
detectors that make the predicate operational: preconditions for trusting a reported verdict, not
additional conjuncts in the definition of fit. This reconciliation chain does not recurse
indefinitely: it terminates at a mechanically consultable primary where one exists, and
otherwise at the human ruling that authorizes the proxy (Section 4.2).

Sufficiency in this theory is therefore claim-relative: a harness is sufficient not when it
establishes absolute correctness, but when it discharges every obligation needed to justify the
bounded claim fit ),& (𝑎), including the evidence that 𝑀!"#$ = 𝑀.

A.2 The semantics of S = P = T = W
Let 𝑆 be the specification, a finite set of declared elements forming the machine-readable core
of the governing representation 𝐺 of A.1; the relations below range over its fields, scenarios,
contract clauses, error mappings, and the other deliverables it names (such as use cases,
endpoints, and reactions), and the converse direction below also over its declared operations
and domain events. Let 𝑃 be the program and 𝑇 the test suite (together, an artifact state 𝑎 of


                                                  45
A.1), and 𝑊 the world. The equality signs are mnemonic: each denotes an agreement relation
defined below, not mathematical identity between 𝑆, 𝑃, 𝑇, and 𝑊. This 𝑆 is the specification in
the verification sense: the declared reference against which the first two families of relations
below (S = P and S = T) check 𝑃 and 𝑇. The specification in Jackson’s analysis [12] is narrower: it
describes only the machine’s behavior at its interface with the problem world and, together
with the properties of that world, serves as a premise from which the requirement is argued to
follow. Our 𝑆 also carries the domain specifications of Section 3.2 and the acceptance
scenarios traced to the requirements. Read informally as three overlapping regions (the
elements the specification declares, the program implements, and the tests exercise), the
mnemonic asks that the three coincide over what 𝑆 governs: every declared element must be
realized in the program and checked by the relation that applies to it, and nothing may be
implemented or tested as a requirement that 𝑆 does not declare, since doing so would add a
requirement no human has ruled on, amending the law (Principle 6). Which elements count as
governed is fixed by the law rather than judged case by case: the specification schemas
determine the kinds of element 𝑆 declares (Section 3.2). Everything else, whether supplied by
the templates (Section 3.4) or written inside a declared element to realize it, is implementation
or verification machinery that adds no governed element, such as mappers, adapters, private
helpers, test fixtures, and test builders, and lies outside this comparison. The first two families
below enforce the S-to-P and S-to-T directions, and the third requires the resulting tests to pass;
the converse direction is blocking only where the families below say so and otherwise stays
outside 𝑀. The machine-decidable portion of the S = P = T = W model comprises three families
of relations:

Structural conformance (S = P): for every structurally declared element 𝑒 ∈ 𝑆, its realization set
𝛿(𝑒) of program elements is non-empty and agree(𝑒, 𝑒 + ) holds for every 𝑒 + ∈ 𝛿(𝑒) along 𝑒’s
journey, where agree is the family of agreement predicates of Section 3.5 (for fields: name, type,
and genuine carriage; for error mappings: the controller gates’ checks; for other named
deliverables, at least presence). The converse direction (nothing realized that is not declared) is
blocking for domain-event records, where an undeclared field enters the permanent log, and for
the aggregate root’s public behavior methods and the events each applies directly; for the other
representations it is an advisory diagnostic and, per A.1, stays outside the fit predicate. This
family carries the portion of S = P that direct structural inspection can decide; conformance of
the program’s behavior to 𝑆 is evidenced only indirectly, through the two families that follow.

Scenario coverage (S = T): for every acceptance scenario 𝑠 ∈ 𝑆 not deferred by the specification
(a specification may defer the tests of an entire test tier, such as its integration tests), its test
set 𝜏(𝑠) ⊆ 𝑇 is non-empty and each test’s assertions are checked against the scenario’s
classified then-clauses; declared contract clauses receive their own generated contract tests.
Conversely, a test that cites a scenario 𝑆 does not declare is blocking; what a correctly cited
test additionally asserts is not checked. Faithfulness beyond this structural matching is a
stated limitation (Section 8).

Execution (𝑇(𝑃) = pass): 𝑃 passes every test in 𝑇 under every configured infrastructure profile.
These three families form the part of 𝑀 that compares the artifact with 𝑆; the rest of 𝑀
compares it with the tradition in 𝐺, which the mnemonic does not name (Section 3.4).

S = W is deliberately not a machine-decidable relation in the methodology. 𝑊 is the primary
problem context of Section 4.2: its demands are not available to the harness as a complete
machine-verifiable representation, but are encountered through lived use and stakeholder
judgment. Formally, the model is therefore three families of machine-decidable relations
enclosed by one judgment reserved by construction to humans. The design theory’s economic
claim, whose cost basis A.3 formalizes, is that routinely discharging the three machine-
decidable relations makes sustained human attention to the fourth affordable.

A.3 The detection-cost bound
For a detector 𝑑, let 𝑐, be the cost of one detection pass, denominated in the scarce resource
of that detector (machine time for gates; human attention for the outer loop), and let 𝐵, be the
sustainable budget of that resource per unit time. The economics of detection impose a simple
upper bound on the sustainable feedback frequency:

                                            𝑓, ≤ 𝐵, /𝑐, .
Each detector class is bounded in its own resource, so the regimes are compared by affordable
frequency rather than by cost. With machine-priced detection, the affordable frequency is high
enough to follow the cadence of artifact change: the inner loop. With human-priced detection,
it is far lower, too low to follow that cadence, so feedback must be scheduled; this is the outer
loop, periodic in the Oregon Experiment’s diagnosis [9] and budgeted in Section 4.3. Sustaining
machine-rate feedback without exceeding the human-attention budget therefore requires the
two detector classes to operate at different feedback cadences; the dual-loop architecture is
our realization of that separation (Table 1, P7).
