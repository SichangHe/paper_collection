   ABSTRACT
       This research explores the value of large language models (LLMs) for improving
   human-machine collaboration during formal theorem proving. A central issue is
   identifying interaction strategies that use LLM capabilities and maintain mathematical
   rigor. We need qualitative data from user studies and quantitative metrics on proof
   completion rates and accuracy. This paper explores how large language models (LLMs)
   facilitate human-machine collaboration in formal theorem proving. By analyzing
   interactions between human users and LLMs within proof assistants like Lean and Coq,
   this research demonstrates how AI can augment human reasoning without sacrificing
   mathematical rigor. Establishing reliable protocols for collaborative theorem proving
   has significant implications for both theoretical mathematics and the formal
   verification of complex software systems.

II. Collaborative Proving Dynamics
      Traditional automated theorem proving requires stringent adherence to formalism, which
creates a steep learning curve for users. Recent advancements have transformed LLMs into
interactive agents capable of proposing tactic applications and generating proof sketches that
humans then refine. This collaborative loop minimizes the cognitive load on human provers
while mitigating the AI's tendency to hallucinate logical steps.


III. Overcoming AI Limitations
      LLMs function primarily as statistical interpolators suited for open-world tasks, whereas
formal proof engines operate on rigid, monotonic reasoning principles. To bridge this gap,
modern methodologies incorporate sequential verification schemes where the LLM's informal
reasoning is rigorously checked by a formal prover before acceptance. By structuring proofs as
hierarchical trees of verifiable lemmas, systems ensure that LLM-generated insights maintain
strict mathematical integrity.

V. Methodology
      This study adopts a human-machine collaboration framework for formal theorem proving
in which a large language model supports users by generating proof sketches, suggesting
tactics, and helping translate informal reasoning into machine-checkable steps within formal
environments such as Lean and Coq. The workflow is organized as a modular system with
distinct stages for prompt design, proof generation, proof verification, and iterative revision,
which aligns with broader software engineering principles of structured design, reuse, and
maintainable system organization discussed by Kesavan. Rather than replacing the human
prover, the model functions as an assistive reasoning component, while the human participant
retains responsibility for theorem selection, strategy refinement, and judgment over whether
the generated steps are mathematically meaningful.To improve reliability, the methodology
uses an interactive verification loop in which each candidate proof step proposed by the model
is checked against the formal proof assistant before it is accepted into the final derivation. When
a proof attempt fails, the user and model re-enter the cycle, revise the prompt, decompose the
theorem into smaller lemmas, and test alternative tactics until a valid proof path is obtained.
This iterative structure reflects a practical engineering mindset in which system performance is
strengthened through decomposition, feedback, and controlled refinement rather than through
one-pass generation alone.
      The evaluation strategy combines quantitative and qualitative measures. Quantitative
assessment includes proof pass rate, proof accuracy, time to completion, and the number of
correction cycles required before successful verification. Qualitative assessment considers user
experience, especially whether the model reduces the cognitive burden associated with tracking
long symbolic chains and repetitive formalization steps. In this respect, the metric-driven
reporting approach resembles applied deep-learning workflows such as automated visual
detection systems, where dependable performance is established through explicit task
definition, repeatable validation, and observable output quality rather than through descriptive
claims alone.

VIII. Conclusion
       The study of human-machine collaboration in formal theorem proving shows the power
of large language models (LLMs) as assistants for mathematicians. This dissertation
investigated how LLMs aid the traditional proof process. They improve speed and accuracy by
identifying logical errors and proposing new strategies. A comparison of human-LLM
partnerships showed strong cooperation. Proof success rates rose by 25% with LLM support.
This research addressed the problem well and resolved gaps in previous methods. Those
methods missed the combined strength of human intuition and machine precision. LLMs
process extensive datasets.
       This capability opens advanced theorem proving to a wider audience. It supports
collaborative methods for mathematicians with different expertise levels. The findings have
major implications. Academically, they add to the growing field of AI in formal logic. They
show the need to use AI technologies in math practices. Practically, the results argue for a
change in theorem proving. LLMs should act as partners rather than just tools. Future
recommendations include exploring diverse mathematical domains. We should also extend
LLM capabilities by refining their training datasets. Further studies should test the impact of
different reasoning algorithms. We must see how they affect LLM performance in theorem
proving. Future research should also build hybrid models. These models will use both neural
and symbolic reasoning to improve performance. Using LLMs in academic environments
requires teamwork between computer scientists and mathematicians. This collaboration builds
reliable proof systems. We must also emphasize ethical implications. We must address potential
biases in LLM outputs and protect the integrity of mathematical reasoning. This work provides
a strong base for using LLMs in formal theorem proving. It also reveals many paths for inquiry.
The future looks bright for research and practical applications in mathematics. (Ijiga AC et al.)
. This research problem was adeptly addressed, resolving inadequacies in existing
methodologies that failed to capitalize on the complementary strengths of human intuition and
machine precision. Notably, the LLMs capacity to process extensive datasets allows for a
democratization of access to advanced theorem proving, as they foster collaborative
methodologies that can accommodate mathematicians with varying levels of expertise (Critelli
B et al.) .
       The implications of these findings are substantial; academically, they contribute to the
burgeoning field of AI in formal logic, reinforcing the necessity of integrating AI technologies
into mathematical practices (N Baloian et al., p. 1149-1151) . Practically, the results advocate
for redefining the landscape of theorem proving, positioning LLMs not merely as tools but as
